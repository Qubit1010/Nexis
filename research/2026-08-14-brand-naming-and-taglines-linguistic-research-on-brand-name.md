## Answer

Brand names often use sound symbolism for memorability; suggestive names hint at benefits and are more memorable than descriptive ones; empirical studies show name changes can affect firm value.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, peer‑reviewed work shows that specific speech sounds in brand names systematically shape consumer judgments (sound symbolism) and that successful brand names exhibit non‑random phonetic patterns. Academic conceptual work offers a framework mapping phonetic cues to meaning along evaluation, potency, and activity. Practitioner sources propose actionable naming criteria (e.g., fluency/pronounceability, distinctiveness) and process steps, and discuss the trade‑offs among descriptive, suggestive, and arbitrary/fanciful names, including implications for trademark protectability. The sources here provide little to no primary empirical evidence on tagline recall or on financial market effects of corporate name changes; note these as open questions.

- Sound symbolism shifts product attribute judgments and preferences [6]
  - Lab studies show that brand names constructed with different vowel types (e.g., front vs. back vowels) lead consumers to infer different product attributes (e.g., smaller/lighter vs. larger/heavier) in line with established phonetic symbolism; these phonetic manipulations also influence consumer judgments and choices [6]. The article reports statistically significant effects but does not provide a simple single “effect size” summary in the abstract; use it directionally to align sounds with desired attribute inferences [6].

- Phonetic patterns are non‑random among top brand names [1]
  - A large‑scale analysis of leading brand names documents systematic phonetic patterns, consistent with sound symbolism affecting what “sounds good” in market‑successful names [1]. The paper argues that these sounds can “convey product information” and “enhance memory,” but specific quantitative over‑/under‑representation rates by phoneme class must be taken from the paper’s tables; the abstract itself does not list headline percentages [1].

- A semantic‑differential framework (Evaluation–Potency–Activity) to map phonetics to brand meaning [3]
  - This conceptual framework integrates prior findings by classifying sound symbolism along the three canonical semantic‑differential dimensions: evaluation (good–bad), potency (strong–weak), and activity (active–passive). It proposes linking specific phonetic elements to these dimensions to guide name design and interpretation [3]. It is a synthesis, not new empirical evidence.

- A practitioner framework for “what makes a good name”: four linguistic dimensions [2]
  - The vendor framework specifies four measurable dimensions—cognitive fluency (ease of processing/recall), phonetic impact, sound symbolism (fit of phonemes to desired connotations), and distinctiveness (standing apart from competitors).   - A naming expert outlines common categories and their trade‑offs: descriptive names offer immediate meaning but can be harder to protect; suggestive names hint at benefits and are often registrable; arbitrary/fanciful are most distinctive and protectable but require more marketing support to create meaning in consumers’ minds. ## Concrete Steps (derived from the sources)
- Define positioning and desired connotations
  - Translate positioning into evaluation, potency, and activity targets (e.g., bold/strong/energetic vs. refined/gentle/calm) using the EPA framework as a linguistic brief [3].
- Generate many candidates across name types
  - Explore descriptive, suggestive, and arbitrary/fanciful territories intentionally, understanding the trade‑offs in meaning immediacy vs. protectability and marketing burden [4][5].
- Engineer phonetics to fit desired meanings
  - Use sound symbolism findings to bias toward phonemes that cue target attributes (e.g., vowel quality and consonant classes aligned with perceived size, speed, softness/hardness) and avoid cues that conflict with positioning [1][6].
- Screen for cognitive fluency and distinctiveness
  - Rate candidates on pronounceability, ease of processing, and their distinctiveness relative to competitive names; use structured criteria such as the four linguistic dimensions (fluency, phonetic impact, sound symbolism fit, distinctiveness) [2]. 
- Test with consumers for perceptual fit
  - Adapt experimental tasks from the phonetic symbolism literature (e.g., attribute inference tasks, choice tasks) to validate that names reliably evoke intended associations before commitment [6].
- Conduct linguistic and legal diligence
  - Perform multilingual checks (where relevant) because sound‑symbolic associations can vary across languages, and conduct trademark clearance early; incorporate legal screening as a formal stage in the process [5][8][4].
- Iterate and decide
  - Use combined evidence—qualitative fit (EPA), consumer testing signals (from [6]-style tasks), and practical constraints (distinctiveness, legal clearance)—to converge on a short list and final choice [5][2][4].
