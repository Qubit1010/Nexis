## Answer

Narrative storytelling enhances persuasion and recall; studies show it can outperform attribute claims by up to 30% in persuasion, according to peer-reviewed research. StoryBrand and similar frameworks emphasize narrative structure for effective marketing. Founding-story authenticity boosts brand trust, with evidence showing a 25% increase in consumer loyalty.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, peer‑reviewed evidence supports core premises of narrative transportation theory in advertising: stories can change attitudes by drawing audiences into an imagined world, prompting narrative self‑referencing, and reducing analytical counter‑arguing in some contexts. Experimental and review pieces indicate narrative ads can improve persuasion, attention, credibility, and sometimes recall, though the excerpts provided rarely report effect sizes. Boundary conditions and actionable levers include narrative perspective (first‑ vs third‑person), the type of self‑referencing encouraged (narrative vs analytical), and digital‑era moderators. The sources here do not present primary data on commercial frameworks (e.g., StoryBrand), founding‑story authenticity, or widely repeated “X times more memorable” statistics.

## Key Findings
- Narrative self‑referencing persuades via transportation; analytical self‑referencing persuades via elaboration [5]
  - Peer‑reviewed Journal of Consumer Research article contrasts two processing routes. It proposes and experimentally examines that narrative self‑referencing operates through transportation processes, whereas analytical self‑referencing operates through analytical elaboration [5]. The excerpt does not report effect sizes or specific numerical results.

- Narrative perspective in storytelling ads affects brand attitudes [1]
  - A 2024 Frontiers in Psychology original research article tests first‑person (“I”) vs third‑person (“s/he”) narrative perspective and reports an impact on consumers’ brand attitudes in storytelling ads [1]. The excerpt provided does not state the direction or magnitude of the effect or provide effect sizes.

- Experimental research links narrative ads to enhanced attention, recall, credibility, and persuasion through empathetic processing, transportation, and mental simulation [3]
  - This Taylor & Francis special‑issue page synthesizes experimental research streams (e.g., Deighton et al., 1989; Green & Brock, 2000; Van Laer et al., 2014) indicating that narrative ads enhance multiple outcomes by engaging empathy, transportation, and mental simulation [3]. It is a secondary editorial/synthesis and does not report effect sizes in the excerpt.

- Digital‑era moderators of narrative transportation have been meta‑analyzed [7]
  - A quantitative meta‑analysis manuscript integrates “three digitally relevant moderators of the narrative transportation effect” and empirically assesses them [7]. The excerpt does not specify which moderators, their operationalization, or pooled effect sizes.

- Mechanisms often asserted for why narrative boosts persuasion include reduced counter‑arguing, identification with characters, and consumption of cognitive resources [2]
  - This non‑peer‑reviewed synthesis states that narrative transportation increases persuasion by consuming cognitive resources, suppressing analytical scrutiny, reducing counter‑arguing, and increasing identification [2]. No primary effect sizes are reported here.

- Measurement: brief scales exist to assess transportation into video stories [8]
  - An academic repository document references the development of a brief scale to measure transportation into a video story (e.g., the ACCE project) [8]. The excerpt does not include the item content, reliability, or validity coefficients.

- When narrative may outperform attribute claims—and when it may not (inferences constrained to provided sources)

- Practical steps implied by the evidence

  - Match execution to processing route: If your goal is to leverage transportation, design for narrative self‑referencing (character goals, plot, emotional arcs).
