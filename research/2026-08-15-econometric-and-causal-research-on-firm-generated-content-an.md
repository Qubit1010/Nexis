## Answer

Firm-generated content in social media can increase customer spending and cross-buying, with effect sizes around 0.0285 and 0.0128 respectively. Long-term brand communication effects are often under-measured, while owned-media content can produce incremental revenue. Peer-reviewed evidence is preferred over agency opinions.

## Key Findings
- Firm-generated content can positively influence customer behavior and firm outcomes (peer-reviewed working paper) [1]
  - A customer-level empirical study links exposure to a retailer’s Facebook posts (FGC) with subsequent behavioral outcomes (e.g., purchasing, cross-buying). The authors use econometric modeling to estimate the impact of FGC on individual customers’ behaviors and report positive effects of FGC on customer activity and value. The paper also examines heterogeneity by content characteristics and customer responsiveness. While the report describes economically meaningful effects, it is the within-study evidence (single firm, customer-level panel) rather than a broad meta-estimate [1].
- Owned social media shows small but statistically significant effects on sales on average across studies (peer-reviewed meta-analysis) [2]
  - A meta-analysis integrates results across multiple studies of brands’ owned social media. It concludes that owned social media has a positive effect on both social media engagement and sales, with the average sales impact being statistically significant but modest in magnitude relative to engagement effects. The study also analyzes moderators (e.g., category, platform) to explain variation across contexts [2].
- Long-term versus short-term effects: practitioner frameworks emphasize structural time-series models and mediation through brand equity (vendor/practitioner sources) [3][4]
  - A measurement framework proposes using vector autoregressions with exogenous inputs (VARX), brand-equity mediators, lag structures, and impulse response analysis to estimate long-term causal effects of brand marketing. Concrete steps include preparing time-series for brand KPIs and sales, fitting VARX, diagnosing stability, and computing impulse-response functions to quantify long-run multipliers [3].
  - Agency guidance highlights media mix modeling (MMM), geo/holdout experiments, and calibration to attribute long-term brand effects that do not show up in immediate performance KPIs. Recommended steps include building a longitudinal data set, specifying ad-stock/saturation, testing lag structures, validating with experiments, and reporting both short- and long-term multipliers [4].
- Branded content: theoretical grounding is strong; causal sales evidence is heterogeneous and limited (peer-reviewed review article) [7]
  - A review of branded content outlines its rise, definitions, and theoretical bases (e.g., Elaboration Likelihood Model; Persuasion Knowledge Model) and discusses the diverse manifestations of branded content across platforms and publishers. It emphasizes heterogeneity in outcomes and challenges in isolating causal sales effects, given the variety of formats and disclosure practices [7].
- Social media’s contribution to brand outcomes is widely discussed in industry commentary, but causal econometric estimates are sparse in these sources (industry article, non–peer-reviewed review, and an academic-style manuscript not confirmed peer reviewed) [5][6][8]
  - An industry article from 2011 discusses how social media can affect brand marketing but does not provide causal econometric effect sizes on sales or customer behavior [5].
  - A recent preprint-style systematic review compiles strategy recommendations and reports mixed findings on performance and ROI, underscoring the need for longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs; however, as a preprint, it is not peer reviewed and aggregates heterogeneous sources with varying rigor [6].
  - An online manuscript reports that social media marketing improves brand awareness and reach, but it is not a peer-reviewed causal study and relies on descriptive or correlational evidence [8].
- Incrementality of owned media revenue: evidence suggests positive, incremental effects in specific contexts, but average effects are small (peer-reviewed sources) [1][2]
  - Customer-level estimates in one retail context indicate that FGC exposure is followed by increases in customer activity and value, consistent with incremental effects for that firm’s audience [1].
  - The meta-analysis indicates a positive sales effect of owned social media on average, yet small in magnitude, implying incrementality exists but is typically modest across contexts and studies [2].
- Selection and reporting bias: directly quantified evidence is limited in the provided sources; practitioner pieces warn implicitly via methodology choices (mixed) [2][3][4][6][7]
  - The meta-analysis aggregates published findings and discusses moderators; it provides the most rigorous synthesis among the sources. While meta-analyses often test for publication bias, this specific concern is not detailed in the description available here. Thus, any claim about publication/selection bias testing in that meta-analysis cannot be made without consulting the full text [2].
  - Practitioner sources emphasize using designs (MMM, geo experiments, holdouts, lag structures) intended to mitigate attribution bias and selection on observables, implicitly acknowledging that naive reporting (e.g., vendor dashboards) can be biased [3][4]. The preprint review and the branded content review both note challenges in causal inference and measurement credibility in this space, but they do not offer a quantified estimate of selection bias in firm-reported metrics [6][7].

## Evidence Quality
- Stronger evidence
  - Peer-reviewed or academically grounded: the customer-level econometric study of FGC and customer behavior (MSI working paper by academic authors) provides within-firm causal estimates using econometric methods [1]. The meta-analysis synthesizing owned social media effects across studies is peer reviewed and provides the best available cross-context estimate of average effects, with explicit attention to moderators and standardized effect sizes [2]. The branded content review in a peer-reviewed journal provides theoretical framing and documents heterogeneity and measurement challenges [7].
- Moderate to limited evidence
  - Non–peer-reviewed or practitioner sources: the long-term measurement playbooks (VARX, MMM steps) are methodologically coherent but are agency/practitioner guidance rather than validated causal estimates in a specific context [3][4]. The 2011 industry article is descriptive and not designed as causal econometric evidence [5]. The preprint review [6] and the ResearchGate manuscript [8] synthesize or report associations but are not peer-reviewed causal studies.
- Consensus vs. contested
  - Converging themes: Owned social media can affect sales, but average effects are small; engagement effects are larger; credible long-term effect estimation requires longitudinal modeling and/or experiments; causal identification is challenging [1][2][3][4][7].
  - Heterogeneity and disagreement: Single-firm, customer-level studies can find economically meaningful increases in behavior/value following FGC exposure [1], whereas cross-study meta-analytic results indicate small average sales effects across brands and categories [2]. This reflects differences in context, measurement, and identification rather than a direct contradiction.

## Open Questions
- Quantifying selection/reporting bias in firm- or platform-reported social metrics: None of the provided sources offers a direct, quantified estimate of such bias; more work using pre-registered designs, audits, or within-platform experiments would be needed [2][3][4][6][7].
- Long-term multipliers specific to owned social media and branded content: Practitioner frameworks describe how to estimate them, but the provided sources do not report standardized long-term elasticities for owned media across categories [2][3][4][7].
- External validity: How effects vary by platform algorithms, disclosure rules for branded content, and category dynamics remains insufficiently pinned down in the materials provided [2][7].
