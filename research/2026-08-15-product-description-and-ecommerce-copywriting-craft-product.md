## Answer

Product page copy should highlight benefits, not just features, and use concise, compelling language. Focus on customer benefits, readability, and SEO. Use frameworks like AIDA to structure copy for maximum impact.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, guidance on ecommerce product copy converges on a few themes: lead with benefits, support with clear specifications, format for scanning, and address buyer doubts. The materials are practitioner blogs and vendor content rather than peer‑reviewed research; they provide qualitative frameworks, checklists, examples, and a few concrete numbers (for example, typical description lengths and marketplace character limits), but no causal effect sizes for conversion lift. Where statistics are mentioned (e.g., macro ecommerce market projections), the primary data source is not provided in the cited articles.

## Key Findings
- Product page copy is often neglected yet is a controllable lever for conversion and brand personality; the recommendation is to turn descriptions into clear, scannable, benefit‑led copy instead of bland spec lists [1].
- Structure: front‑load benefits and key information so shoppers see “what’s in it for me” quickly; then provide supporting details and specs. This “benefit‑first, details‑second” ordering is explicitly recommended [1][8].
- Scannability: use short chunks and bullet points to surface critical facts and specifications without forcing readers through long paragraphs; checklists stress making copy easily skimmable [1][7][8].
- Specifications alongside benefits: do not omit specs; reposition them to support decision‑making after (or adjacent to) the benefit claim. Several sources criticize copying spec sheets verbatim and advise linking each feature/spec to the outcome it enables [1][6][7][8].
- Tone and brand voice: descriptions are a place to “sprinkle brand personality,” provided clarity is not sacrificed [1][3][8].
- Concrete length guidance:
  - Typical ecommerce product descriptions often fit in 60–80 words, per a practitioner guideline [5].
  - Amazon listings commonly allow about 2,000 characters for the description area, per the same source [5].
  - These are formatting/constraint guidelines, not experimentally derived optima [5].
- Marketplace copy specifics:
  - Amazon: plan for character‑limited fields and concise benefit‑plus‑spec language within ~2,000 characters for the description [5].
  - Shopify: the sources provided do not include Shopify‑specific copy constraints or requirements; guidance remains platform‑agnostic [1][3][4][5][6][7][8].
- DTC brand product copy examples: the example roundups show “best‑in‑class” patterns used by top brands—benefit‑led headlines, concrete details, sensory/active language, and concise bullets. These are presented as qualitative exemplars rather than quantified case studies [1][3][8].
- Category page copy: the sources emphasize general product‑page techniques (benefits first; scannability; specs), but do not supply dedicated, data‑backed prescriptions unique to category pages beyond applying the same clarity and scanning principles to help shoppers orient and filter choices [1][4][7][8].
- Teardowns and example‑based lessons: curated examples highlight tactics that recur on high‑performing product pages (e.g., front‑loaded benefits, concrete problem–solution framing, specific details vs. vague adjectives), but they are not paired with measured conversion deltas in these articles [1][3][8].
- Checklists and stepwise methods:
  - A 7‑step process for writing product descriptions is presented, including word‑count and marketplace‑length guidance; it is positioned as a practical workflow rather than a tested framework with measured outcomes [5].
  - Additional checklists emphasize appealing to buyer motivations and ensuring all essential decision information is present and easy to find [7].
- Language tactics:
  - Use active, benefit‑oriented language and clearly state how the product solves a real‑world problem; front‑load the most important facts to respect shoppers’ limited attention [8].
  - Avoid listing specs in manufacturer order; re‑sequence to align with the shopper’s top questions and doubts before purchase [6].
- Macro statistic caveat: one article states ecommerce will reach $8 trillion by 2026; no primary source is provided within that article, so the figure cannot be validated from the given source set [4].

## Evidence Quality
- Study types: All sources are practitioner/agency/vendor blogs or a Medium post—no peer‑reviewed studies, randomized experiments, or meta‑analyses are included in this set [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].
- Quantification: Aside from formatting guidance (e.g., 60–80 words; ~2,000 Amazon characters) [5] and an uncited market projection [4], the sources provide qualitative recommendations, examples, and checklists—no reported effect sizes or controlled test results linking specific copy tactics to conversion lift.
- Consensus vs. contested:
  - Consensus across sources: lead with benefits; keep copy scannable; include specs tied to outcomes; use clear, active language; respect marketplace constraints [1][3][5][7][8].
  - Disagreements: None explicit in these sources; differences are mainly emphasis (e.g., brand personality versus brevity), not direct contradictions [1][3][5][7][8].
- Unsupported or untraceable stats: The $8T market projection is reported without a primary citation in the article itself [4]; no other statistics in these sources claim quantified conversion effects attributable to copy tactics.
