## Answer

The claim that human attention span has fallen to eight seconds lacks credible primary sources; it originated from a fabricated statistic in a 2015 Microsoft report, which cited an unreliable source. No peer-reviewed evidence supports this figure.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, several popular marketing “facts” lack traceable primary evidence. The widely repeated “8‑second human attention span” has no primary study behind it; journalism could not verify the cited institutions ever produced such a finding, and multiple sources label it a myth [1][2][3][4][7]. Eye‑tracking research from Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) shows users do scroll, but devote about 80% of their attention to content above the fold and 20% below it—undercutting “nobody scrolls” while quantifying the above‑the‑fold advantage [6]. Claims that “people read only 20% of a web page” are not supported in these sources; the 20% figure refers to attention below the fold in NN/g’s data, not the fraction of a page read [6]. For personalized email subject lines, Mailchimp advises they may increase open rates but provides no specific percentage uplift in this benchmark resource [8]. The “$300 million button” anecdote, A/B testing power/replication problems, and the base rate for non‑replicating “wins” are not addressed in the provided materials.

## Key Findings
- Human attention span “8 seconds” claim
  - Investigative journalism traced the oft‑cited references (NCBI/US National Library of Medicine and the Associated Press) and found no record of research backing the statistic; the listed sources could not locate any such study [7].
  - Multiple analyses characterize the “8‑second attention span” as a myth lacking scientific basis [1][2][3][4].
    - [1] explicitly calls the 8‑second claims “scientifically baseless.”
    - [2][3][4] describe the number as widely repeated without a primary source.

- Scrolling behavior and “below the fold”
  - NN/g original research (eyetracking) finds users “spend 80% of their time looking at information above the page fold” and “only 20% of their attention goes to content below the fold.” Users do scroll, but attention is concentrated above the fold. The article notes an update with newer eyetracking data on bigger monitors [6].

- “People read only 20% of a web page”
  - None of the provided sources support this specific claim. The only 20% figure identified is NN/g’s estimate of attention share below the fold, which is not the same as “reading only 20% of a page” [6].

- Personalized email subject lines
  - Mailchimp’s benchmark guidance: “Personalized emails may increase open rates for most users,” with practical advice to use merge tags in subject lines; no specific percentage uplift is claimed in this source [8].

- Practical steps implied by the evidence in these sources
  - Prioritize critical content above the fold because that region receives about 80% of users’ attention; still design for scrolling since users do scroll and allocate the remaining 20% of attention below the fold [6].
  - If testing email subject lines, personalization (e.g., merge tags for name/location) is a reasonable tactic to try; do not assume a fixed uplift without testing your audience, because this source gives no percentage benchmark [8].
  - Regarding attention‑span narratives, avoid using the 8‑second figure in strategy decks; none of these sources provide a primary study supporting it [1][2][3][4][7].
