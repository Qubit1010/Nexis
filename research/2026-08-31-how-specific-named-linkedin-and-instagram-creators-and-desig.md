## Answer

LinkedIn creators sequence carousels with one idea per slide, front-loading the promise on the cover, and reserving the CTA for the penultimate slide. Instagram carousels use specific, attention-grabbing covers and follow frameworks like checklists or before/after breakdowns. Successful carousels balance engagement and clear messaging.

## Detail
- Platform context (LinkedIn, 2026):  
  - LinkedIn “carousels” are now document/PDF posts (the multi‑image carousel is gone). This affects sequencing because the first page must function as a cover slide with a strong hook; the remainder should read like a concise deck; the last page serves as the CTA or summary [6]. Oktopost’s 2026 best‑practices similarly anchor on document/PDF carousels for B2B, reinforcing that the deck‑like structure is the norm this year [3].

- Platform context (Instagram, 2026):  
  - Instagram rewards pause, swipe, save, and share behaviors; carousel storytelling should be engineered to drive those specific actions:  
    - Cover slide = “pause” (a promise/hook).  
    - Early/middle slides = “swipe” (a progression that keeps curiosity or value flowing).  
    - Mid/late slides = “save/share” (checklists, steps, or frameworks worth returning to and passing along).  
    - Close = CTA or share‑prompt (explicit ask to save/share/follow/DM/etc.) [1].  
  - For writers, 2026 idea sets and prompts exist to adapt carousels to narrative content, indicating continuing appetite for slide‑by‑slide storytelling on Instagram this year (published Feb 12, 2026; updated Apr 15, 2026) [5].

- Sequencing technique that creators and designers converge on in 2026 (from practitioner‑style guides and teardown frameworks):  
  - Cover/Slide 1: Clear hook that promises a payoff; visually indicate there’s more to see (e.g., an arrow pointing to the next slide button) to encourage swipes [7].  
  - Middle slides: One idea per slide with a natural progression; recommended structures include:  
    - Problem → mistake → fix  
    - Insight → steps → example  
    - Case study → strategy → result  
    These structures clarify pacing and keep attention by revealing the solution step‑by‑step [8].  
  - Closing slide: Specific CTA—comment, save, share, follow, click, or DM—so the narrative resolves into an action [7].  
  - This hook → reveal → resolve arc maps cleanly to Instagram’s behavior model (pause → swipe → save/share/act) [1].

- How “one idea per frame” is paced across a published‑style flow (applied technique):  
  - Slide 1 (promise): State the core transformation or question users care about (hook) [7].  
  - Slide 2 (context): Set stakes or common mistake (why this matters) [8].  
  - Slides 3–5 (steps): Reveal the process one step per slide; keep text short and readable [7][8].  
  - Slide 6 (proof/example): Show an example or mini case so the abstract advice becomes concrete [8].  
  - Slide 7 (application): How to implement today (checklist or quick start) to earn saves [8][1].  
  - Slide 8 (resolve/CTA): Summarize the win and ask for a save/share/comment/follow [7][1].  
  - Notes: The above is a technique distilled from sources; it is not a creator‑authored teardown. It operationalizes the behaviors Instagram values and the deck‑like expectations on LinkedIn [1][6][7][8].

  - Promise: Slide 1 should make an explicit, scannable claim (e.g., “X in Y steps” or “Stop doing Z—do this instead”) and visually cue swipes [7][8].  
  - Resolution: Last slide should close the loop by restating the transformation or summarizing the steps and directing the next action (save/share/comment/follow) [7].
