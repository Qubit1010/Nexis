# Diagnosing and auditing an existing business strategy: Richard Rumelt's good strategy bad strategy and the kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, the hallmarks of bad strategy including fluff and goal setting mistaken for strategy, strategy audit frameworks and scorecards, why strategies fail at execution, and the questions that expose a strategy with no real choices in it.

*mode: general | depth: deep | 2026-08-04*

---

## Answer

A good strategy diagnoses the central challenge, defines a guiding policy, and implements coherent actions; bad strategy confuses goals with strategy and lacks real choices. Strategies often fail due to poor diagnosis and avoiding hard decisions. To audit, check for these hallmarks of bad strategy.

## Summary
This report outlines how to diagnose and audit an existing business strategy using Richard Rumelt’s “kernel” of good strategy—diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action—and the common hallmarks of bad strategy such as fluff and confusing goals with strategy. It translates these ideas into a practical audit checklist, highlights why strategies often fail in execution, and offers questions that expose strategies with no real choices in them, grounded in the cited sources.

## Key Findings
1) Good strategy has a kernel made up of three parts: a clear diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy to address it, and coherent actions to carry out that policy [1][5][6][8].  
2) Effective strategy concentrates resources on one or two critical issues—focus and leverage (asymmetry)—so that a small, well-directed push achieves outsized impact [4][7].  
3) Bad strategy is marked by four recurring problems: fluff (empty buzzwords), failure to face the real problem, mistaking ambitious goals/targets for strategy, and bad strategic objectives (lists that don’t address the critical issue) [5][6][8].  
4) Much of what passes for “strategy” is actually a mix of ambition, vision, and financial targets—not strategy—confirming the risk of goal-setting masquerading as strategy [2].  
5) A practical audit can be built directly from Rumelt’s kernel: test whether there is a sharp diagnosis, a coherent guiding policy, and a set of coordinated actions aligned to that policy; absence of any component is a red flag [1][5][6][8].  
6) Execution commonly fails when objectives are a laundry list, actions are not coordinated, and the guiding policy is vague—i.e., when the hallmarks of bad strategy are present [5][6][8][2].  
7) Questions that surface whether a strategy makes real choices include: What one or two critical issues are we focusing on now? What coherent actions follow from that focus? If the “strategy” is only aspirations or KPIs with no diagnosis/policy/actions, there are no real choices in it [4][7][2][6].  
8) The sources present Rumelt’s kernel as a de facto strategy audit framework; however, they do not provide a separate quantitative “scorecard” beyond this checklist-style evaluation [1][5][6][8].

## Detail
What a good strategy is (the kernel)
- Rumelt’s kernel specifies three interlocking components:  
  - Diagnosis: a clear-sighted definition of the critical challenge (what’s really going on) [1][5][6][8].  
  - Guiding policy: an overall approach to cope with/overcome the challenge (how you intend to deal with it) [1][5][6][8].  
  - Coherent actions: a coordinated set of steps that implement the policy (what you will do, aligned and mutually reinforcing) [1][5][6][8].  
- This kernel is the core lens for evaluating any existing strategy: can you see a crisp problem framing, an intelligible approach, and a tight set of reinforcing actions? If any part is missing or weak, the strategy is weak [1][5][6][8].

Focus, leverage, and real choices
- The central concept emphasized in summaries is focus plus asymmetry: identify the few pivotal issues where concentrated effort yields outsized results—a “small, well-directed push” that moves the system more than a large, diffuse effort [4].  
- Good strategies narrow attention to one or two critical issues and align actions accordingly; this focusing inherently involves making choices and trade-offs [7][4].

Hallmarks of bad strategy
- Repeated patterns of bad strategy include:  
  - Fluff: high-sounding, content-free statements [5][6][8].  
  - Failure to face the problem: avoiding the real obstacles or challenges [5][6][8].  
  - Mistaking goals for strategy: substituting ambition, targets, or vision statements for a strategy [5][6][8][2].  
  - Bad strategic objectives: a long, incoherent list of initiatives that doesn’t tackle the crux issues [5][6][8].  
- Reviews stress that many “strategies” are merely collections of ambition, vision, and financial KPIs—signals of goal-setting mistaken for strategy [2].

Auditing an existing strategy (kernel-based checklist)
- Diagnose:  
  - Does the strategy clearly identify the critical challenge(s) we face now? Is there a succinct problem framing? [1][5][6][8]  
- Guiding policy:  
  - Is there a plainly stated approach for dealing with that challenge—one that implies where we will concentrate resources? [1][5][6][8]  
- Coherent actions:  
  - Is there a short, coordinated set of actions that logically flow from the guiding policy (no grab-bags or unrelated initiatives)? [1][5][6][8]  
- Focus test:  
  - Does the strategy pinpoint one or two critical issues to focus on? If it tries to do everything, it likely reflects no real choices [7][4].  
- Bad-strategy filter:  
  - Strip out fluff and ask: once the slogans are gone, what is left—diagnosis, policy, and coherent actions, or just goals/targets? If only the latter remain, it is not a strategy [5][6][8][2].

Why strategies fail at execution
- When the “strategy” is a list of objectives not anchored in diagnosis/policy, execution fragments into disconnected efforts; teams can’t coordinate without a clear guiding policy [5][6][8].  
- Vague or platitudinous guidance (fluff) creates ambiguity, so actions don’t cohere; laundry lists of “priorities” dilute effort and stall progress [5][6][8].  
- Confusing financial targets or high-level ambitions with strategy deprives operators of the how (policy) and the what (coherent actions), leading to poor execution despite bold aspirations [2][6].

Questions that expose strategies with no real choices
- What one or two critical issues are we focusing on right now, and why these? If you can’t name them, there is likely no focus [7][4].  
- What is our guiding policy for these issues—i.e., the approach that shapes what we will and won’t do? If none is articulated, choices are unclear [1][5][6][8].  
- What specific, mutually reinforcing actions are we taking because of this policy? If actions don’t align, there’s no coherent choice pattern [1][5][6][8].  
- If we remove high-level goals and KPIs, what remains? If nothing remains beyond targets or vision, the “strategy” consists of goals mistaken for strategy [2][6][8].

Strategy audit frameworks and scorecards
- The sources consistently use Rumelt’s kernel as a practical audit framework: evaluate diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions, plus focus on the few critical issues [1][5][6][8][7].  
- While the kernel functions as a checklist-like audit tool, none of the sources provide a separate, quantitative “strategy scorecard” beyond this structured evaluation [1][5][6][8].

## Gaps / Caveats
- The sources are secondary summaries and reviews; they do not provide a formal, numeric scorecard or metrics system for auditing strategy beyond the kernel/checklist approach [1][5][6][8].  
- They emphasize Rumelt’s principles but offer limited step-by-step operationalization or case data on execution outcomes; reasons for execution failure are discussed conceptually rather than empirically [2][5][6].
