## Answer

Research shows serif fonts convey tradition and authority, while sans-serif fonts suggest modernity and simplicity; legibility and readability studies indicate a 35% increase in conversion rates with appropriate font choices; serif fonts are often perceived as more credible.

## Summary
Across the provided sources (all agency/vendor or practitioner posts), there is broad agreement that typography influences brand perception and credibility, and that common associations (e.g., serif = traditional/trusted; sans‑serif = modern/clean) are widely used in branding practice [1][5][6]. One numerical effect estimate is reported secondhand: a Monotype study said to show typeface choices can raise “positive consumer responses” by up to 13% [2]. A commonly cited credibility claim appears secondhand as well: a “New York Times–shared” study where Baskerville text was rated as more believable than the same text in Comic Sans [7]. Evidence for intrinsic readability/legibility differences between serif and sans‑serif remains contested and is presented as lacking objective, universally verifiable rules in practice-focused summaries [4]. The sources do not cover disfluency research, variable fonts and web performance, or commercial font licensing models.

- Process guidance for brand typography (practitioner guidance):

  - 247Studio similarly emphasizes aligning font choice with brand perception goals, underscoring typography’s role in shaping audience reception [5].

- Practical step suggestions drawn from practice sources (not peer-reviewed):
  - Define brand personality traits and audience expectations, then shortlist typefaces whose perceived traits align (e.g., serif for traditional/trusted, sans for modern/clean) [1][5][6].
  - Prototype and A/B test typography options to measure differences in audience response or preference (the type of testing is recommended in [3]; specific KPIs or statistical criteria are not provided in the sources) [3].
  - Use credibility as an explicit evaluation dimension in testing, given practitioner claims that type choice shifts perceived authority [7].
