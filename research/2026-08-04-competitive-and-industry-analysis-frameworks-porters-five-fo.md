## Detail
Porter’s Five Forces and what they assess  
- Core idea and purpose: The Five Forces determine an industry’s competitive structure and its profitability; while industries differ, the underlying drivers of profitability are consistent and analyzable through these forces [2].  
- Forces and application:  
  - Threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors together define competitive intensity and how profits are distributed within an industry over time [5].  
  - Guidance on using the model includes understanding each force in detail and applying the framework to evaluate industry attractiveness and strategic positioning [3].  
- Use in practice: A practical orientation to competitor analysis references Five Forces as a real‑world tool to structure the analysis of markets and rivals [4].  
- Investor lens: Investment analysts begin with industry analysis and view company performance as largely shaped by industry competitive structure, directly tying Five Forces‑style structural thinking to long‑term business outcomes [8].

Identifying indirect and substitute competitors  
- Mapping competitors requires distinguishing direct from indirect rivals. Direct competitors offer similar products to the same customers; indirect competitors address the same customer problem with a different solution, which aligns with the “threat of substitutes” concept in Five Forces [7]. This distinction is a critical first step in building a comprehensive competitive landscape [7].

Using industry analysis to judge structural attractiveness  
- Since the Five Forces link structure to profitability, analysts use them to evaluate whether an industry’s conditions are conducive to sustainable profits or intense competition that erodes margins [2][5]. Investors similarly prioritize industry structure in judging a business’s prospects [8]. The sources frame attractiveness in terms of structural forces and their implications for profit potential, rather than in terms of valuation outcomes [2][8].

How consultants frame industry analysis  
- Consulting work commonly starts with “What’s going on in this industry?” and applies industry analysis frameworks to understand markets and competition before moving to company‑specific strategy [1]. The emphasis is on structuring the problem and scanning competitive dynamics systematically [1].

What’s covered versus not covered in these sources  
- Covered in depth: The logic of Five Forces, its role in explaining profitability and shaping analysis, and the importance of clarifying competitor types (direct vs indirect/substitute) [2][3][5][7][8].  
- Not covered in depth: Specific competitive positioning matrices, PESTEL/macro‑trend scanning procedures, systematic competitive intelligence gathering methods, and an explicit catalog of criticisms or limitations of Five Forces (beyond general application guidance) [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].
