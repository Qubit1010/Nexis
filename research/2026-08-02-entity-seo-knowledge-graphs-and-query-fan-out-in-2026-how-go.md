## Detail
- Defining “entity” for SEO and AI contexts
  - General references concur that an entity is “something that exists as itself,” often defined as something with real existence; the concept includes abstractions as entities in philosophical and legal contexts [2][3][5][7]. This broad scope underpins why brands, people, organizations, products, and even abstract concepts appear as nodes in knowledge graphs.

- How Google and LLMs build entity understanding
  - Scale and training linkage: The 2026 entity SEO guide reports Google’s Knowledge Graph contains 500B+ facts on 5B+ entities and that Gemini AI is trained on this graph—connecting KG-scale factual grounding to LLM behavior [1].
  - AI search mechanics: An analysis of AI search platforms explains that LLM-based systems expand user queries into many sub-questions, retrieve evidence at the passage level, and assemble composite answers [6]. This workflow depends on robust entity recognition to segment, match, and synthesize information correctly across sources [6].
  - Industry consensus on centrality: A 2026 video discussion frames brand entity SEO and knowledge graphs as the foundation of SEO now, emphasizing that search engines, LLMs, and AI assistants rely on entity understanding to power results and citations [8].

- Knowledge Graph optimization, Wikidata, and disambiguation
  - Wikidata: The 2026 guide explicitly points to Wikidata, noting that an entry there “needs no notability,” which makes it a practical venue to seed canonical facts for an entity profile that AI systems can consume [1].
  - sameAs: The guide states sameAs is officially supported by Google, signaling that it is a recognized method to declare identity links across a brand’s canonical profiles (e.g., site, social, authoritative listings) to reduce ambiguity [1].
  - Structuring facts for AI understanding and citation: Another practitioner guide emphasizes defining a brand with verifiable facts and a structured representation to enable AI systems to “understand and cite accurately,” reinforcing that clearly sourced, structured facts improve how brands appear in AI-generated responses [4].

- Query fan-out and content coverage
  - Fan-out and passage retrieval: AI search engines “expand queries into dozens of sub-questions, retrieve at the passage level, and assemble answers” [6]. This means systems do not only match whole pages—they preferentially extract specific, relevant passages.
  - Implications: Given this behavior, content strategies need to ensure that likely sub-questions are directly answered in precise, verifiable passages, with facts that are easy for entity recognition systems to associate to the right entity [6][4]. This alignment increases the odds of inclusion in AI-assembled answers.

- Knowledge Graphs and Knowledge Panels
  - The sources reviewed center on Knowledge Graph optimization and entity grounding but do not provide procedural detail on Knowledge Panels (e.g., triggers, review processes, or panel management). Where Knowledge Panels are implied in practice by KG presence, the materials here focus instead on entity facts, Wikidata, and structured signals like sameAs [1][4][8].

- Brand mentions without links and AI citation
  - The practitioner guidance focuses on verifiable facts and structured representation to achieve accurate AI citation [4]. None of the provided sources directly quantify or confirm the impact of unlinked brand mentions on AI citation behavior. The emphasis remains on structured, disambiguated facts and recognized identity links [4][1].

## Gaps / Caveats
- Knowledge Panels: The sources do not detail how Google triggers, updates, or governs Knowledge Panels, nor how KG changes map to panel appearances; conclusions about panel mechanics cannot be drawn from these materials [1][4][8].
- Wikipedia vs. Wikidata roles: While Wikidata is explicitly discussed (including the “no notability” claim), the materials do not lay out a comparative or procedural role for Wikipedia as a KG source; any assertions beyond noting Wikidata’s accessibility would be speculative [1].
- Unlinked brand mentions: No source here substantiates whether or how unlinked mentions affect AI citations; the only concrete guidance is to supply verifiable facts and structured signals for disambiguation [4][1].
- Training specifics: Beyond the statement that Gemini is trained on Google’s Knowledge Graph, the materials do not detail the training methodology or relative weighting of KG data in LLM outputs [1].
- Schema specificity: sameAs support is explicitly stated; other schema types that practitioners often use (e.g., Organization) are not specified in these sources, so their effects cannot be asserted here [1][4].
- Quantification of fan-out: We are told engines expand to “dozens of sub-questions,” retrieve at passage level, and assemble answers; the sources do not provide formal metrics or evaluation benchmarks for coverage breadth or retrieval thresholds [6].
