## Answer

Source credibility comprises expertise and trustworthiness; expertise relates to perceived knowledge, while trustworthiness involves honesty. Research shows that demonstrated experience and credentials enhance perceived competence. Peer-reviewed studies emphasize the importance of these dimensions in effective communication.

## Summary
This report synthesizes the provided evidence on source credibility and perceived expertise in communication, focusing on: credibility scales and their dimensions; cues of expertise and trustworthiness; author identity/byline; organizational vs individual voice; demonstrated experience; and how audiences judge authority in online information. The strongest points of convergence across these sources are: (a) credibility is commonly modeled as multidimensional, with competence/expertise and trustworthiness as core dimensions; (b) author credentials/qualifications are salient credibility cues; (c) in misinformation contexts, source labels/credibility cues are not a simple or consistent “fix.” Across the provided materials there are few quantifiable effect sizes; several items are conceptual reviews or tertiary summaries. Where the sources do not cover a requested topic (e.g., organizational vs individual voice, numeric effect sizes), that gap is noted explicitly.

## Key Findings
- Credibility as a construct and historical context (tertiary summary)
  - Definition: “a term commonly used to imply a communicator’s positive characteristics that affect the receiver’s acceptance of a message.” [1]
  - Historical emphasis: Academic study intensified in the 20th century with special emphasis during World War II propaganda efforts. [1]
  - Evidence type: Wikipedia summary (not peer‑reviewed). [1]

- Credibility dimensions and measurement tradition (peer-reviewed lineage summarized on an academic page)
  - Named framework: Early multidimensional measurement of source credibility in communication research dates to 1966 (McCroskey, “Scales for the measurement of ethos”). These scales measured two dimensions: competence and trustworthiness. [6]
  - Field trajectory: “Measurement of source credibility has been a concern … for over 40 years,” with many subsequent studies expanding on these scales. [6]
  - Numbers available: Year (1966); “over 40 years” of work. No effect sizes reported on this page. [6]

- Review of credibility measures across decades (peer‑reviewed)
  - Scope: A peer‑reviewed article reviews credibility measures in media research from 1951 to 2018, indicating a long and diverse tradition of operationalizing the construct. [5]
  - Numbers available: Coverage spans 1951–2018. No effect sizes or specific dimensional counts are stated in the provided excerpt. [5]
  - Implication: Use of multiple, evolving measures complicates direct comparison across studies; selection and reporting of measures matters. [5]

- Source credibility in misinformation research (review/primer; peer‑review status of venue not stated on the provided page)
  - Core conclusion: “Source credibility isn’t a simple fix for fake news.” The review points to conceptual and methodological flaws that limit clear conclusions about the size or consistency of source-credibility effects in misinformation contexts. [2]
  - Disagreement with classical intuition: Contrasts with the general presumption that more credible sources are uniformly more persuasive, suggesting mixed effects and design issues in this subfield. [2]
  - Numbers/effect sizes: None reported in the provided excerpt. [2]

- Online information credibility and expertise levels (tertiary summary of a JCMC study)
  - Finding (as summarized): Perceived credibility of online health information reflects both the audience’s topic knowledge and the source’s expertise; the study divides expertise into three levels, and emphasizes information that is free of bias and includes the author’s qualifications. [7]
  - Numbers available: “Three levels” of expertise are reported in the summary; no effect sizes provided. [7]
  - Evidence type: Wikipedia summary of a Journal of Computer‑Mediated Communication study (tertiary; not a direct reading of the peer‑review). [7]

- Social media eWOM perspective on source credibility (doctoral dissertation; committee‑reviewed, not a journal peer review)
  - Focus: Assesses source credibility on social media through an electronic word‑of‑mouth (eWOM) communication lens, situating credibility judgments in user‑generated contexts. [4]
  - Implication: Platform and eWOM context shape how audiences infer credibility online, though no specific numeric effects are available in the excerpt. [4]

## Concrete Steps (anchored to the provided sources)
- Use established multidimensional scales when measuring credibility
  - Employ measures rooted in the McCroskey tradition that separate competence (expertise) and trustworthiness; document item sets and reporting transparently. [6]
  - When reviewing or selecting measures for studies, consult syntheses of measures used from 1951–2018 to ensure conceptual fit and comparability. [5]
- Do not over‑rely on source labels in misinformation contexts
  - Design interventions and evaluations that do not assume credibility tags will suffice; attend to methodological pitfalls flagged by the misinformation review (e.g., potential confounds between source and message). [2]
- Make author identity and credentials salient in online content
  - Prominently display qualifications near the byline and aim for content that is free of bias, as emphasized in online health credibility discussions. [7]
  - Highlight credentials (a named factor influencing perceived credibility in practitioner guidance). [8]
- Situate social media credibility work in eWOM contexts
  - When assessing or building credibility on social platforms, recognize that credibility judgments occur within eWOM dynamics; design studies and practices that account for platform‑ and community‑specific cues. [4]
