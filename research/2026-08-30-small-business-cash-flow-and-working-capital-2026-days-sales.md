## Answer

In 2026, small firms face cash flow issues due to late payments, with 82% failing from cash flow problems; invoice financing helps bridge gaps, but only 44% of SMBs have access to traditional credit.

## Summary
The 2026 sources provided consistently show that cash flow, not profitability, is the binding constraint for many small businesses. Widespread cash flow disruptions, late customer payments, and financing frictions are recurring themes, while multiple sources stress that even profitable firms can run out of cash due to working‑capital timing issues such as slow receivables collection [7][3][1][5].

## Key Findings
1) Cash flow disruptions are pervasive: 88% of small businesses experience cash flow disruptions, yet fewer than one‑third take mitigation steps like tracking expenses or streamlining payroll [7].

2) Cash flow is a leading cause of failure: one study cited reports that 82% of small businesses fail due to cash flow issues [8].

3) Profitability ≠ liquidity: businesses can be profitable on the income statement but still run out of cash because of timing mismatches in receivables, inventory, and payables; this dynamic commonly triggers payroll stress, delayed tax payments, and short‑term borrowing (e.g., credit cards) to plug gaps [3][1][5].

4) Late payments are a major driver: a 2026 compilation aggregates up‑to‑date data on late customer payments and related financing pain points facing small firms [2].

5) Credit access and financing frictions persist: 2026 roundups present small‑business cash flow statistics and financing challenges, including figures drawn from Federal Reserve sources, underscoring ongoing lending and working‑capital gaps for SMBs [4][2].

6) Invoice financing/factoring is discussed as a tool to bridge cash gaps caused by slow‑paying customers and tight liquidity [6].

## Detail
Cash flow versus profitability and the working‑capital cycle
- Multiple sources emphasize that “making money” on the income statement can coincide with being cash‑poor in the bank because cash conversion depends on the timing of collections, inventory turnover, and supplier terms. That mismatch is a primary reason otherwise profitable firms strain to meet obligations [3][5]. 
- On‑the‑ground symptoms include payroll pressure, delayed tax remittances, and reliance on high‑cost short‑term credit (e.g., credit cards) when incoming cash lags—patterns explicitly described by a CPA firm’s account of common cash‑flow pitfalls [1]. 
- Together these underscore the centrality of receivables timing (often tracked as DSO) in small‑business liquidity, even when accounting profitability looks sound [3][5][1].

Prevalence of disruptions and failure attribution
- Cash flow disruptions are near‑universal (88% of small businesses), yet fewer than one‑third report taking routine mitigations like expense tracking or payroll streamlining, indicating a sizable execution gap in basic cash‑management practices [7]. 
- As to failure attribution, one study widely cited in 2026 materials indicates 82% of small businesses fail due to cash flow problems, highlighting cash management as a dominant failure mode in contrast to “lack of profitability” per se [8]. The provided sources reinforce this by noting that profitable businesses still fail when cash receipts don’t align with outflows [3][5][1].

Late payments, DSO, and large‑customer behavior
- A 2026 roundup compiles “up‑to‑date data points” on late customer payments and rising costs, situating late payment as a central cause of cash‑flow stress for small firms [2].

- Similarly, although late payment is acknowledged as pervasive, the sources here do not quantify the specific impact of “late payment by large customers” versus smaller buyers [2].

Access to credit and the lending gap

Invoice financing/factoring
- Factoring (a form of invoice financing) is explicitly presented as a way small businesses address cash‑flow gaps tied to slow‑paying customers by advancing cash against accounts receivable [6]. 
- Broader guidance on fixing cash shortfalls—tightening collections, improving forecasting, and aligning terms—appears in practical playbooks on resolving the profit‑cash disconnect and avoiding common cash‑flow pitfalls [3][5][1].

Operational mitigations
- Concrete steps highlighted include better expense tracking and payroll streamlining (where adoption remains relatively low), along with broader cash‑flow planning and AR discipline to avoid the recurring cycle of shortfalls [7][5][1]. These measures target the core drivers—receivables timing and expense control—identified across the sources.
