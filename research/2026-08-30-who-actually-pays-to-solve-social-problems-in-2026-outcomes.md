# who actually pays to solve social problems in 2026: outcomes-based funding, social impact bonds, pay-for-success contracts, government social technology spending, philanthropy and foundation technology budgets, nonprofit software market size

## Answer

Governments, private investors, and foundations fund social problem solutions in 2026 through pay-for-success contracts, social impact bonds, and technology spending. Nonprofits also contribute, with growing software market budgets.

## Summary
Across outcomes-based models such as Pay for Success and social impact bonds, private and philanthropic investors typically provide upfront capital, while governments (and in some variants, donors) act as “outcome payers” who reimburse or pay returns only if agreed results are achieved [3][2][8][7]. The sources explain the mechanics, roles, benefits, and limitations of these approaches, but they do not provide 2026-specific spending totals for government social technology, philanthropic technology budgets, or the nonprofit software market size [1]-[8].

## Key Findings
1) In outcomes-based funding (Pay for Success, social impact bonds), governments most often serve as “outcome payers,” paying only when independently verified results are achieved; private and sometimes philanthropic investors supply upfront capital and take performance risk [3][2][8][7].  
2) Pay for Success emerged amid constrained public budgets and dissatisfaction with traditional inputs-based funding, aiming to shift public spending toward paying for measurable results [2][4][5].  
3) Impact bonds/PFS show potential benefits (focus on outcomes, risk transfer) and significant limitations (complexity, measurement challenges, equity and accountability concerns); evidence of effectiveness is mixed [3][6].  
4) Philanthropy can play multiple roles: co-investor providing upfront capital, provider of guarantees or catalytic funds, or outcomes payer in donor-led variants; concrete examples include philanthropic and bank capital being repaid only if results are met [7][3].  
5) “Pay for Success” is an umbrella for outcomes contracting that includes, but is not limited to, social impact bonds; terminology and structures vary across contexts [1][8].  
6) The provided sources do not report 2026 dollar amounts for outcomes-based contracts, government social technology spending, philanthropy/foundation technology budgets, or the nonprofit software market size [1]-[8].

## Detail
Who pays—and when—in outcomes-based models
- Social impact bonds (SIBs) and related Pay for Success (PFS) contracts organize four core actors: an outcome payer (commonly government), investors (private and/or philanthropic) who provide working capital, service providers who deliver programs, and an independent evaluator; payments flow to investors and delivery partners only if pre-defined outcomes are achieved [3][1][8].  
- SSIR emphasizes that interest in SIBs/PFS rose as government social welfare funding diminished, with the promise of attracting private investment into social services—shifting who fronts the money and tying repayment to results [2].  
- Concrete examples show philanthropy and private finance as the upfront funders who receive results-based payouts if targets are met (e.g., J.B. & M.K. Pritzker Family Foundation and Goldman Sachs) [7]. Government Outcomes Lab further clarifies that impact bonds include variants where governments or donors are the outcome payers; in donor-led versions (development impact bonds), philanthropic or aid funders make outcome payments [3].  
- The White House’s description underscores that “Pay for Success” refers to a range of outcomes contracting approaches—of which SIBs are one subtype—reinforcing that “who pays” depends on model design (government or donor outcome payers; private/philanthropic capital at risk) [8].

Why these models are used
- Multiple sources frame PFS as a response to undercapitalized nonprofits, stagnant social outcomes, and fiscal pressure on public budgets, motivating a reorientation from paying for activities to paying for results [2][4][5].  
- Social Finance describes PFS as tools that “drive resources toward results” through defined outcomes and data use, clarifying the intent to improve measurability and accountability in social spending [1].

Benefits and limitations (and what that implies for who pays)
- Potential benefits highlighted by Government Outcomes Lab include a focus on outcomes, performance management, and risk transfer from governments to investors who front the capital [3].  
- At the same time, the academic literature flags concerns: complexity, transaction costs, measurement and evaluation challenges, and potential inequities in which populations or services get funded under results-linked contracts [6]. These limitations temper expectations that PFS/SIBs will broadly replace traditional public or philanthropic funding; rather, they are tools for specific contexts [3][6].  
- Taken together, the sources indicate that, in these models, public (or donor) funds “pay for success” while private/philanthropic investors “pay for risk” upfront—clarifying that both sectors pay, but at different times and contingent on outcomes [3][2][7].

What the sources do not cover (especially for 2026)
- None of the sources provide 2026-specific totals for: government social technology spending; philanthropy or foundation technology budgets; nonprofit software market size; or the aggregate scale of outcomes-based funding in 2026 [1]-[8].  
- The materials are primarily definitional, conceptual, and case- or policy-oriented (with benefits/limitations and roles), rather than market-sizing or budget datasets [1]-[8].

## Gaps / Caveats
- No 2026 spending figures: The provided sources do not quantify government social technology spending, foundation technology budgets, or the nonprofit software market size for 2026 (or any other year) [1]-[8].  
- Limited scope on technology: The sources focus on outcomes-based financing mechanics and policy rationale; they do not address “who pays” for social-sector technology specifically [1]-[8].  
- Evidence base: Government Outcomes Lab and the public health literature indicate mixed evidence and substantial limitations; the sources do not resolve debates about cost-effectiveness or scalability of PFS/SIBs across sectors [3][6].  
- Generality of “who pays”: While the roles are clear in SIB/PFS models, the sources do not compare the relative magnitude of outcomes-based payments versus traditional appropriations or philanthropy in 2026 [1]-[8].
