# Market research and market sizing methodology: how to calculate TAM, SAM and SOM, top-down versus bottom-up sizing, where the input data actually comes from, primary versus secondary research methods for a small business, the most common market sizing errors and inflated numbers, and how investors actually judge a market size claim.

## Answer

To calculate TAM, SAM, and SOM, use top-down or bottom-up methods. Top-down uses macro data; bottom-up uses unit economics. Investors prefer credible, defendable estimates.

## Summary
This report synthesizes practical guidance on market sizing for small businesses: how to define and calculate TAM, SAM, and SOM; when to use top-down versus bottom-up approaches; where inputs come from; how to incorporate primary versus secondary research; common errors; and how investors evaluate market size claims. Across sources, the most credible estimates triangulate top-down and bottom-up methods and validate assumptions with primary research from real buyers, with explicit scoping and assumptions throughout [2][3][5][6][7][8].

## Key Findings
1) TAM, SAM, SOM are nested scopes of demand; TAM is total demand, SAM is the portion your business can serve, SOM is the share you can realistically capture in the near term [1][4][6].  
2) Top-down sizing starts from a published or known market total and narrows by filters (e.g., customer segments, geography, channels) to SAM and then SOM [6][1].  
3) Bottom-up sizing multiplies the number of potential customers/accounts by annual revenue per customer (or price), producing a grounded TAM/SAM that reflects your pricing and targetable base [7][8].  
4) Investors place more weight on bottom-up or triangulated estimates; credible analyses use multiple methods and make every assumption explicit [2][3][5].  
5) A robust workflow builds independent top-down and bottom-up estimates, then validates key assumptions (demand, adoption, willingness to pay) with primary research from verified target buyers; the figure that survives both is most credible [5].  
6) Input data for bottom-up comes from counts of potential customers/accounts and realistic annual revenue per customer; top-down draws on existing market totals before applying serviceability filters [7][8][6].  
7) Common pitfalls include conflating TAM with SAM/SOM, relying on unfiltered top-down numbers, assuming arbitrary market share, and failing to align the market definition with the actual customer and go-to-market path [2][3][1][4].  
8) For small businesses, primary buyer interviews/surveys are crucial to pressure-test adoption and pricing assumptions; secondary/top-down figures should be used as a cross-check rather than as the sole basis [5][2].  
9) Market sizing is a strategic exercise about clarity of who the customer is and how you reach them—not about producing the biggest number; investors judge market claims on this clarity and on methodological rigor [2][3].

## Detail
- Definitions and scope
  - TAM estimates the total demand for a product or service in a defined market; SAM narrows to the portion your business can actually serve; SOM is the realistically obtainable share over a planning horizon [1][4][6]. Aligning these scopes to your true customer and channels avoids inflated numbers [1][2][4].

- Top-down vs bottom-up methodologies
  - Top-down: Begin with a recognized total market, then narrow with explicit filters (e.g., verticals, geographies, customer sizes, channel constraints) to derive SAM and then SOM. This approach is fast but prone to overestimation if filters are not precise [6][1].  
  - Bottom-up: Calculate by multiplying the number of target accounts/customers by your annual price or revenue per customer; this ties estimates to your actual GTM and pricing, often making it more credible to investors [7][8][3].  
  - Triangulation: The strongest practice is to develop both an independent top-down and a bottom-up estimate, then reconcile them; investors favor analyses using multiple methods with explicit assumptions [2][3][5].

- Where the input data actually comes from
  - Bottom-up inputs: the count of potential customers/accounts and the expected annual revenue per customer (e.g., price, ARPU) [7][8].  
  - Top-down inputs: an external or published total for the broad market that you then pare down via serviceability criteria [6].  
  - Assumptions: Across both approaches, state and test assumptions explicitly (e.g., adoption rates, eligibility filters, pricing) to maintain credibility with investors [2][5].

- Primary vs secondary research for a small business
  - Primary research: Validate your assumptions directly with verified target buyers—e.g., gauge real demand, adoption likelihood, and willingness to pay; use this to confirm or adjust both top-down and bottom-up estimates [5].  
  - Secondary research: Use existing market totals for top-down framing and as a cross-check; avoid relying solely on a single secondary figure without serviceability adjustments or validation [6][5][2].  
  - Workflow: Build both estimates independently and pressure-test with primary buyer input; use the number that stands up to this validation [5].

- Common errors and inflated numbers
  - Conflating TAM with SAM/SOM—presenting the broadest market as your immediate opportunity [1][4].  
  - Unfiltered top-down sizing—starting with a large published market and not applying tight serviceability filters [6][3].  
  - Arbitrary “we’ll get x%” share assumptions—SOM must be tied to GTM reach and validated adoption/pricing data [2][3][5].  
  - Single-method estimates and hidden assumptions—credible sizing uses multiple methods and makes all assumptions explicit [2][5].  
  - Vague customer definition—lack of clarity on who the buyer is and how you reach them undermines credibility with investors [2][3].

- How investors judge market size claims
  - They look for strategic clarity on the actual customer, reachable segments, and the GTM path, not just a big number [2][3].  
  - They prefer bottom-up or triangulated estimates that connect accounts, pricing, and adoption to an obtainable SOM; explicit, testable assumptions and primary validation increase confidence [2][3][5].  
  - Position your SAM and SOM as the core story, using TAM as context; this aligns with how early-stage investors assess practical opportunity [2][3].
