## Answer

Rich media formats and interactive cues significantly boost social media engagement, with photos outperforming videos and links in comments and shares. Emotional and informational content both drive engagement, with videos often leading in shares. Post length varies in effectiveness, but concise, engaging content tends to perform better.

## Summary
Across peer-reviewed reviews and field studies, engagement with social media content is consistently linked to (a) emotional features—especially arousal and discrete emotions—(b) content characteristics such as vividness (e.g., images/video) and interactivity, and (c) message purpose (informational vs emotional/entertaining). However, effects are not uniform: what increases likes can differ from what drives comments or shares, and modality effects (image/video/link) vary by context and metric. Evidence on “ideal” post length and universal modality advantages is sparse in the peer‑reviewed sources provided. Non–peer‑reviewed summaries and frameworks (e.g., STEPPS) are widely used in practice but should be treated as directional rather than definitive. Where sources disagree, we preserve the disagreement below.

## Key Findings
- What “engagement” means and how it’s measured varies across studies (peer‑reviewed) [6]
  - Engagement is multi-dimensional (cognitive, emotional, behavioral) and operationalized with heterogeneous metrics (e.g., likes, comments, shares/retweets, clickthroughs, time on page). This variation makes cross‑study comparisons of effect sizes difficult and can explain mixed results across content features [6]. 
  - Numbers/effect sizes: The review catalogs measures but does not report pooled effect sizes [6].

- Emotions and arousal are central predictors of sharing; information-heavy content can suppress sharing for video ads except in risky contexts (peer‑reviewed study summarized by AMA) [7]
  - Two independent field studies (video ads; 11 emotions; >60 ad characteristics) found: information‑focused content had a significantly negative effect on sharing, except in risky contexts; high‑arousal positive emotions (e.g., inspiration, amusement) and surprise increased sharing; sadness decreased sharing unless paired with elements like hope/rescue [7].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: Directional findings are reported; specific coefficients are not provided in the summary [7].

- Content characteristics (vividness, interactivity) and emotional valence/arousal relate to behavioral engagement, but findings are mixed across platforms and metrics (peer‑reviewed literature review) [1]
  - Vividness (e.g., images/video) and interactivity (e.g., questions, polls) are frequently associated with higher engagement, yet effects differ by outcome (likes vs comments vs shares) and context; emotional content—especially high arousal—often predicts sharing more than low‑arousal emotions [1].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: The review synthesizes directions of effects; comparable numeric effect sizes are not provided [1].

- Message content features in media‑industry posts predict engagement types differently (peer‑reviewed empirical field evidence) [2]
  - Using media‑industry data, the study links specific message features to engagement outcomes; informational and emotional elements do not uniformly affect reactions, comments, and shares, indicating the need to tailor content features to the targeted engagement behavior [2].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: The accessible document confirms significant relationships but does not provide extractable coefficients in the provided excerpt [2].

- Branded content forms that prompt “active” engagement emphasize participation, relevance, and identity expression (peer‑reviewed, qualitative) [3]
  - Event-based diaries and interviews indicate that audiences report higher propensity to comment/share when content invites participation (e.g., challenges), aligns with personal relevance/values, or enables self‑expression; overtly promotional content tends to dampen active behaviors [3].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: Qualitative evidence; no quantitative effect sizes are reported [3].

- Emotional and temporal features carry predictive signal for likes/comments, but evidence is algorithmic and pre‑peer‑review (preprint) [4]
  - A machine learning approach shows that emotional and temporal variables can predict engagement metrics (likes, comments), implying these features encode meaningful variance; however, results are correlational/predictive rather than causal and are not peer‑reviewed [4].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: The summary indicates predictive success; detailed performance metrics are not available in the provided excerpt [4].

- Informational vs emotional content and engagement type (non‑peer‑reviewed synthesis) [5]
  - Aggregated summaries suggest informational content can facilitate both active and passive engagement, while emotional content primarily drives passive engagement, implying a mix may be optimal depending on goals [5].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: Not provided; this is a secondary synthesis, not a primary peer‑reviewed estimate [5].

- Virality framework used in practice: STEPPS (non‑peer‑reviewed, practitioner content referencing academic work) [8]
  - The STEPPS framework (Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, Stories) is commonly applied to design for sharing/virality. It emphasizes emotional arousal and practical value among other levers [8].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: None provided in the practitioner piece [8].

- Image vs text vs link performance: peer‑reviewed synthesis notes “vividness,” but platform‑wide numeric advantages are not established here (peer‑reviewed review; contested/varies) [1]
  - Vividness (e.g., images/video) is often—but not always—linked to higher engagement. Effects can depend on the specific engagement metric and platform norms; the review notes mixed findings rather than a universal advantage [1].
  - Numbers/effect sizes: Not provided; effects vary [1].

- Post length and engagement: not established in the provided peer‑reviewed sources
  - None of the provided peer‑reviewed sources report numeric effects of caption/post length on engagement outcomes. Widely repeated “optimal length” claims are therefore not traceable within this evidence set [1][2][3][6].

## Evidence Quality
- Peer‑reviewed syntheses: 
  - Systematic/literature reviews [1][6] offer convergent themes (importance of emotional arousal; roles of vividness/interactivity) but stress heterogeneity in definitions and measures, limiting comparable effect sizes and contributing to mixed findings.
- Peer‑reviewed empirical studies:
  - Field studies of video ad sharing summarized by a professional association (AMA) draw on multiple large‑scale datasets and theory‑driven hypotheses [7]. Findings are robust across two studies but specific to video ads and sharing behavior.
  - Media‑industry field evidence links specific message features to engagement outcomes [2], but extractable numeric coefficients are not available here; generalizability may be sector‑ and platform‑specific.
  - Qualitative diaries/interviews illuminate mechanisms for “active” engagement in branded content but do not quantify effect sizes [3].
- Preprint/algorithmic:
  - Predictive modeling of engagement from emotional/temporal features suggests signal in these variables but is correlational and not peer‑reviewed [4].
- Practitioner/aggregator:
  - Consensus-style summaries and LinkedIn posts provide accessible frameworks (e.g., STEPPS) and high-level takeaways but are not substitutes for primary, peer‑reviewed estimates [5][8].

Areas of consensus:
- Emotional arousal (especially high‑arousal positive emotions and surprise) is associated with higher sharing/virality [1][7].
- Content characteristics such as vividness and interactivity often raise engagement, though not uniformly across likes, comments, shares [1][2].
- Engagement is multi-dimensional; effects differ by metric and platform [6][1].

Areas of disagreement/variation:
- The impact of informational content: Some evidence suggests information‑heavy content suppresses sharing for video ads except under risk contexts [7], while aggregator‑level summaries suggest informational content can facilitate active engagement more broadly [5].
- Modality effects (image/video/link) and their magnitudes are mixed across contexts and metrics; no universal numeric advantage is established in the provided peer‑reviewed sources [1].

Widely repeated but untraceable statistics:
- Common “best practice” claims (e.g., universal percentage uplifts for images vs text, or optimal character counts) are not supported by traceable primary evidence within the provided sources. Where such statistics appear in practice literature, the primary, peer‑reviewed basis is not evident here [1][2][3][6][5][8].

## Open Questions
- Quantitative modality effects by platform and metric: What are the platform‑ and outcome‑specific effect sizes for images vs video vs links on likes, comments, and shares? The provided sources synthesize directions but do not supply comparable coefficients across platforms [1][2].
- Post length and structure: How does caption length, readability, and narrative structure quantitatively affect different engagement types across platforms? Not addressed in the provided peer‑reviewed sources.
- Causality vs prediction: To what extent do emotional and temporal features causally increase engagement versus merely correlate with audience/contextual factors? Preprint predictive evidence does not identify causal pathways [4].
- Boundary conditions: Under what conditions does informational content boost versus depress sharing outside video ads (e.g., during crises, for news/journalism)? Evidence is suggestive but context‑specific [7][2].
- Transferability: How well do findings from media brands and video ads generalize to other verticals (e.g., B2B SaaS, nonprofits) and newer formats (Stories/Reels/Shorts)? Current sources do not establish external validity.

Concrete, evidence‑aligned steps (with caveats about effect-size uncertainty):
- When your goal is sharing/virality for video‑like content, emphasize high‑arousal positive emotions and surprise; avoid purely information‑heavy executions unless communicating risk/uncertainty where information aids sharing [7].
- Separate goals and metrics (likes vs comments vs shares), and tailor content features accordingly; do not assume a feature that lifts likes will also lift shares [6][1][2].
- Incorporate vividness/interactivity (e.g., images, questions, polls), then test by metric and platform because effects are mixed across outcomes [1][2].
- Blend practical value with emotional resonance (consistent with STEPPS), but treat frameworks as heuristics and validate with controlled tests; do not rely on untraceable “X%” rules [8][6].
- Use emotional and temporal features as inputs to predictive targeting and scheduling, while recognizing these are correlational signals that require experimentation for causal claims [4][6].
