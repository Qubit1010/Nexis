## Answer

Startups often fail due to cash flow issues, lack of market need, and poor team dynamics. The failure rate is high, with many citing financial mismanagement as the primary cause. Cash flow problems contribute to nearly 38% of startup failures.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, official U.S. statistics show that roughly 4 out of 5 new business establishments survive their first year, with one‑year survival rates commonly in the high‑70s to low‑80s percent range and variation by location and year [8]. Commentary sources broadly align, noting around 22% first‑year closures in the U.S. and cautioning that widely quoted “90% failure” figures usually pertain to venture‑backed tech startups rather than all businesses [3]. Peer‑reviewed research examines determinants of startup outcomes: an econometric study analyzes the longevity and success of funded startups [2], and a conceptual model emphasizes core competency deficits as a root cause of failure and notes that high failure‑rate claims are pervasive in the literature [7]. A U.S. government–commissioned report highlights undercapitalization (insufficient capital to operate and service debts) as a contributor to business failure for women entrepreneurs [6]. The preprint included surveys financial challenges behind startup failures but is not yet peer‑reviewed [1]. The sources provided do not supply direct empirical estimates on customer acquisition cost (CAC), retention, or cash‑flow metrics as quantified predictors.

## Key Findings
- U.S. one‑year survival rates for new business establishments:
  - The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports one‑year survival rates by year and location with many entries in the high‑70s to low‑80s percent range. Example values in the table include 78.5% (2002) and 80.4% (2003), with other entries such as 82.4% and 83.7% appearing in mid‑2000s rows; the series exhibits location‑level variation [8].
- Framing of “failure rate” statistics and first‑year closures:
  - A practitioner analysis argues that the oft‑cited “90% startup failure rate” refers to venture‑backed tech startups, not all businesses, and states that U.S. SBA data show roughly 22% of startups close in year one [3].
  - The same source asserts that acquisitions succeed at approximately twice the rate of startups, based on its operator experience; this is a non‑peer‑reviewed claim [3].
- Variation by industry, location, and time:
  - A secondary synthesis emphasizes that survival statistics vary significantly by industry, location, and length of operation (no single “one‑size‑fits‑all” rate) [4].
- Venture funding and startup longevity:
  - A peer‑reviewed econometric study analyzes the survival and success of funded startups. It provides an empirical framework linking funding status to longevity and success, though effect sizes are not extractable from the provided excerpt [2].
- Failure causes: competency and early failure patterns:
  - A peer‑reviewed conceptual model proposes that core competency deficits underlie many startup failures and notes that various sources in the literature claim failure rates around 90%, with many failures occurring in very early stages (as cited within that article). These are literature‑based references rather than official statistics in the excerpt provided [7].
- Undercapitalization and failure risk:
  - A U.S. National Women’s Business Council report defines undercapitalization as lacking sufficient capital to conduct normal operations and service debts, and studies its contribution to business failure among women entrepreneurs [6].
- Financial challenges synthesis (preprint):
  - A recent preprint compiles evidence on financial challenges behind startup failures; the version provided is not peer‑reviewed, and specific estimates are not cited here from the excerpt [1].

## Open Questions
- Customer acquisition and retention economics:
  - The provided sources do not report empirical CAC, LTV, or retention‑rate economics. Quantified relationships between CAC/LTV dynamics and survival are not documented in these materials [1]–[8].
- Cash flow as a predictor:
  - Beyond the definition and focus on undercapitalization for women entrepreneurs, quantified cash‑flow–based predictors of failure (e.g., liquidity ratios, burn multiple) are not provided in the excerpts [6]. Additional empirical studies are needed for effect sizes and thresholds.
- Causal impact of funding on survival:
  - While [2] studies funded startups’ longevity, the provided excerpt does not supply effect sizes or causal estimates (e.g., addressing selection into funding). Clear causal inferences and magnitudes remain open from the materials shown [2].
- Disaggregation and external validity:
  - Industry‑specific, region‑specific, and cross‑country survival curves and long‑horizon closure rates (beyond year one) are not shown in these excerpts [4][8].
- Acquisitions vs. de novo startups:
  - The assertion that acquisitions succeed at roughly twice the rate is not peer‑reviewed in the provided materials; independent, large‑sample, official or academic evidence would be needed to validate magnitude and scope [3].
- Definitional scope:
  - Several sources caution that “startup” vs. “small business” vs. “venture‑backed tech” populations differ; standardized definitions and harmonized datasets would improve comparability of failure/survival statistics across studies [3][4][7].
