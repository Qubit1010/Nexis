# Positioning and the unique value proposition: April Dunford's positioning framework, the value proposition canvas, how to find real differentiation versus claimed differentiation, category design versus competing in an existing category, how to test a value proposition with customers, and why most value propositions are undifferentiated feature lists.

*mode: general | depth: deep | 2026-08-04*

---

## Answer

April Dunford's framework emphasizes value-focused differentiation, identifying unique features, mapping them to customer benefits, and targeting specific customer segments. Most value propositions are undifferentiated feature lists, not real customer value. Test value propositions with customers to ensure differentiation.

## Summary
Across these sources, April Dunford’s positioning approach is a repeatable, cross‑functional process that starts with competitive alternatives and works forward to unique capabilities, differentiated value for best‑fit customers, and an intentional choice of market category; sales messaging should be driven by this differentiated value, not features [1][2][3][4][7][8]. The materials offer little on the Value Proposition Canvas and only limited guidance on formal testing; they emphasize that many value propositions fail because they list features instead of clearly articulating why customers should pick you over alternatives [2][3][7].

## Key Findings
1) Dunford’s positioning process: start with competitive alternatives, identify unique attributes/capabilities, translate to value themes customers care about, define best‑fit customers, and deliberately choose a market category; do this in a structured, cross‑functional way [2][4][1].  
2) “No differentiation” is usually an illusion; teams should first clarify what they are positioning against and re‑examine where they actually win versus those alternatives and for which customers [1][5].  
3) Real differentiation is the unique value you deliver versus alternatives; features don’t win deals—clarity on why customers pick you does, and that should drive the sales pitch [3][7].  
4) Category choice matters: pick a category that amplifies your strengths and makes your differentiated value obvious, rather than relying on a rigid template; this is part of the process, not a one‑off label [2][4].  
5) Testing value propositions: the sources emphasize building sales narratives around differentiated value and learning from customer conversations, but they do not provide a formal, step‑by‑step testing protocol [3][8].  
6) Why most value propositions degrade into feature lists: teams lead with features or pricing instead of differentiated value relative to alternatives, leaving buyers unclear on “why pick us?” [2][3][7].  
7) The Value Proposition Canvas is not covered in these sources; any mapping to it is out of scope here [2][3][4][7][8].

## Detail
Positioning framework and process  
- April Dunford frames positioning as a practical, repeatable process rather than a fill‑in‑the‑blanks template. It requires cross‑functional work to align on each element [4].  
- Core elements highlighted across her materials include: identifying the competitive alternatives; surfacing your unique attributes/capabilities; translating those into customer‑relevant value themes; defining who your best‑fit customers are; and choosing a market category that best sets buyer expectations and highlights your strengths [2][4].  
- In practice, she advises starting with what you are positioning against—i.e., the status quo or direct competitors—before asserting differentiation [1][2].

Finding real differentiation vs. claimed differentiation  
- Dunford argues that “no differentiation” is often a diagnostic error. Teams should first define the alternatives customers genuinely consider, then look for the contexts and customer segments where they reliably win [1][5].  
- Real differentiation is expressed as differentiated value—benefits customers care about that alternatives can’t match—rather than as a laundry list of features. This differentiated value should anchor the pitch and the overall positioning [3][7].  
- Cross‑functional alignment helps separate what is truly unique and provable from internal claims; the process focus (versus templates) is intended to surface evidence and reduce hand‑wavy assertions [4].

Category design vs. competing in an existing category  
- Choosing a market category is one explicit step in Dunford’s process: the category should make your strengths obvious and prime buyers to perceive your differentiated value [2].  
- The emphasis is on deliberately selecting the category “frame” that amplifies value, not on rigidly following a formula or on gratuitously inventing a new category. The sources here summarize this as a process decision made to support clarity and fit, with cross‑functional buy‑in [4].  
- These sources do not present a separate “category design” methodology; their guidance stays within the bounds of category choice as part of positioning [2][4].

Value proposition vs. positioning  
- The materials consistently place differentiated value at the center of positioning and sales messaging: why customers pick you over alternatives [3][7].  
- While the general distinction between a value proposition (benefits to a segment) and positioning (relative framing vs. alternatives and in a category) is implied by Dunford’s process, these sources do not provide a formal comparative framework for the two terms. The Value Proposition Canvas specifically is not discussed [2][3][4][7][8].

How to test a value proposition with customers  
- The sources emphasize that sales narratives should be built around differentiated value and clarity on the “why pick us,” implying that customer conversations (e.g., sales calls) are where this clarity pays off and can be refined [3][8].  
- However, they do not offer a step‑by‑step testing protocol (e.g., specific experiments, metrics, or survey designs). Any rigorous testing methodology would be beyond what is provided here [3][8].

Why most value propositions become undifferentiated feature lists  
- Dunford explicitly cautions that features don’t win deals; what wins is being clear about why customers pick you over alternatives—i.e., differentiated value [3].  
- When teams don’t execute the positioning process (competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → best‑fit customers → category), their messaging often defaults to features, which leaves audiences confused about “what is this for?” and “why choose it?” [2][3].  
- Emphasizing “differentiated value” over generic “value” is a recurring theme; without contrasting against alternatives, teams end up with benefits that could apply to anyone and thus fail to persuade [7].

## Gaps / Caveats
- Value Proposition Canvas: None of the sources discuss it; no mappings or how‑tos are covered here [2][3][4][7][8].  
- Testing protocols: The sources stress aligning pitches to differentiated value but do not provide experimental designs, metrics, or detailed testing procedures for value propositions [3][8].  
- Category design (as a standalone discipline): These materials address category choice within positioning but do not present a distinct methodology for designing and evangelizing a new category [2][4].  
- Secondary summaries: Some content comes from summaries rather than primary transcripts or full texts, which may compress nuance [4][7]. Where specifics aren’t in the sources, they are not asserted here.
