# Empirical research on market size estimation and demand forecasting accuracy: peer-reviewed studies on forecast bias and optimism in new venture revenue projections, the accuracy of market potential estimates, reference class forecasting, judgmental forecasting error, and academic evidence on how entrepreneurs systematically overestimate addressable demand.

*mode: scientific | depth: deep | 2026-08-04*

## Answer

Entrepreneurs often overestimate demand due to optimism bias; systematic errors like this can lead to inaccurate market size estimates and demand forecasts. Bias and accuracy are distinct: bias shows systematic over- or under-forecasting, while accuracy measures the overall error magnitude. Academic evidence highlights the frequent overestimation of addressable market potential by new ventures.

## Summary
Across the provided materials, there is consistent guidance on the difference between forecast accuracy (magnitude of error) and forecast bias (systematic over- or under-forecasting) and the importance of tracking both. One academic dissertation examines demand forecast accuracy via case studies in retail contexts, and a practice-oriented paper describes reference class forecasting to mitigate optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation. Practitioner articles provide qualitative recommendations and one set of performance “benchmarks” for sales forecasting accuracy. ## Key Findings
- Forecast accuracy vs. forecast bias are distinct and both must be tracked
  - Forecast accuracy measures how far predictions deviate from actual demand; forecast bias captures the direction of systematic error (consistent over- or underestimation) [1][3][6].  
  - Practitioner guidance emphasizes reducing bias to avoid persistent stockouts or overstock and improving decision quality in demand planning [1][3][6].

- Empirical case studies on demand forecast accuracy (retail/supply chain context)
  - A doctoral dissertation presents “Exploration of and Case Studies in Demand Forecast Accuracy” covering replenishment, point-of-sale, and “bounding conditions,” indicating an empirical, case-based assessment of forecast accuracy in operational settings. The title and scope suggest multi-level analyses (e.g., POS vs. replenishment), though no quantitative results are provided in the accessible description [4].

- Reference class forecasting (RCF) as a debiasing approach
  - A practice-focused paper on RCF argues it curbs optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation by using outcomes from a relevant reference class to produce outside-view forecasts; it cites Daniel Kahneman’s endorsement of RCF’s value for improving forecasting accuracy. The piece frames RCF as a method to address the planning fallacy in real-world planning contexts [7]. No quantitative accuracy improvements are reported in the accessible text [7].

- Practitioner benchmarks for sales forecast accuracy
  - One practitioner article reports benchmark ranges for sales forecast accuracy by organizational maturity: “world-class” 80–95%, “average” 50–70%, and “lagging” below 50%. It also highlights “optimism bias” (overestimation) and “sandbagging” (underestimation) as forms of forecast bias [8]. These benchmarks are not presented as peer-reviewed evidence.

- Methods for demand estimation and forecasting
  - A slide deck outlines approaches to demand estimation (simple and statistical methods) and general steps for estimating demand functions for firms/markets. It does not provide empirical results on estimation accuracy or bias [2].

- Practitioner guidance on measuring and improving sales forecast accuracy
  - Articles discuss how to measure sales and demand forecast accuracy and offer best practices for improvement; they stress tracking both accuracy and bias but offer no peer-reviewed effect sizes or quantified accuracy gains from specific methods [1][3][5][6][8].
