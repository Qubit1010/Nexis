## Answer

Brand positioning defines a brand's unique place in the customer's mind; clearly positioned brands often outperform ambiguous ones, with studies showing 86% higher recommendation rates. Perceptual maps visually compare brands on key attributes. Effective positioning focuses on clear, unique value propositions.

## Summary
Across the provided sources, brand positioning is consistently described as the distinctive place a brand occupies in customers’ minds relative to alternatives, often supported by a formal positioning statement and tools like perceptual maps to visualize competitive space [1][5][7]. Practical “how-to” guidance appears in agency/consultancy materials, outlining steps such as identifying key attributes, drafting a positioning statement, and using perceptual mapping (sometimes grounded in multidimensional scaling, MDS) to assess differentiation [2][4][8]. The materials distinguish brand positioning from broader marketing strategy, though they differ in whether positioning is framed primarily as a mental outcome or as a strategic process [1][6]. None of the sources provide peer‑reviewed causal evidence or quantified effect sizes showing that clearly positioned brands outperform ambiguously positioned ones; the performance benefits asserted are opinion-based without reported primary data [2][4][5][6]. The concept of Category Entry Points (CEPs) is not covered in these sources.

## Key Findings
- Definition of brand positioning
  - Defined as the distinct position a brand holds in the consumer’s mind versus alternatives [1]. Also framed as “the set of perceptions, impressions, ideas and feelings that consumers have for the product compared with competing products” [5] and as how a product is perceived in the mind relative to competitors [7]. No effect sizes or quantitative outcomes reported in these definitions [1][5][7].
- Positioning statement construction (templates and steps)
  - Sources provide templates/guides for constructing a positioning statement and positioning strategy, emphasizing a structured approach (details not quantified) [1][4][7]. These are how‑to recommendations; no outcome metrics or validation data are reported [1][4][7].
- Perceptual mapping and methodology
  - Perceptual/positioning maps are presented as tools to visualize how brands are perceived along key attributes; they are a standard element in brand positioning practice [2][5]. Methodologically, this practice stems from perceptual mapping and multidimensional scaling (MDS) in marketing science and psychometrics originating in the 1960s–1970s (e.g., work by Roger Shepard, Joseph Kruskal, Paul Green), as summarized by [8]. The sources describe creating perceptual maps based on identified key characteristics/attributes [2], with MDS noted as a methodological foundation [8]. No numerical validation metrics (e.g., stress values, fit indices) are reported in these sources [2][5][8].
- Concrete steps commonly suggested in the sources
  - Identify key characteristics/attributes customers use to compare brands [2].
  - Define the brand’s distinct place versus alternatives (the intended position) [1][5][7].
  - Draft a positioning statement and supporting rationale (templates provided in guides) [1][4][7].
  - Create a perceptual/positioning map to visualize competitive space on chosen attributes; the approach draws on perceptual mapping methods and, in some applications, MDS [2][5][8].
  - Iterate based on findings to sharpen differentiation [4]. None of the sources report effect sizes for doing these steps [1][2][4][5][7][8].
- Named frameworks and tests

  - Multidimensional scaling (MDS) as an underpinning methodological framework for perceptual mapping [8].
  - A “Subtraction Test” is mentioned in [1], but the excerpt does not detail its procedure or evidence; no effect sizes are provided [1].
- Relationship between business/marketing strategy and brand positioning
  - One source explicitly distinguishes brand positioning (defining the brand’s unique place in consumers’ minds) from marketing strategy (channels and tactics) [6]. Other sources emphasize the mental position relative to competitors [1][5], indicating a difference in emphasis across sources (mental outcome vs. strategic process). No quantitative comparisons or outcome data are provided [1][5][6].
