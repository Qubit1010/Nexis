# Deliverable Structure — `14-brand-voice.md`

Section order for the brand voice and messaging document.

The bar for this document is **operability**. A voice guide is only worth anything if a
freelance writer who has never met the client can produce on-brand copy from it on their first
try. Every section is built toward that test, and section 7 is the test itself.

---

## 0. What we know

The house opener. `| Fact | Source | Confidence |` covering the inputs: what copy we read,
what the client told us, what came from `13-brand-strategy.md`, what we inferred.

**Fails when** a voice is asserted with no evidence of what the brand currently sounds like.

---

## 1. Voice at a glance

One page a writer can keep open while working. The three or four dimensions with their marked
positions, the five words in, the five words out, and one before-and-after pair.

Write it last.

**Fails when** it is a paragraph of description rather than something usable at a glance.

---

## 2. Voice dimensions

**This is the section that makes the document work or fail.** Voice is defined as positions on
scales, not as adjectives.

Format each dimension as a scale with the brand's position marked and both ends named:

```
Formal  |-----------X-------|  Casual
        We use contractions and plain words. We do not use slang,
        exclamation marks, or first-name-basis familiarity with strangers.
```

Three to five dimensions. Useful ones: formal/casual, serious/playful, direct/diplomatic,
expert/peer, restrained/enthusiastic.

For each, state **what the position rules out on both sides**. A position with no exclusions
on either end is the midpoint, which is not a choice.

**Fails when** it degenerates into adjectives. "Friendly but professional" is the canonical
failure: it excludes nothing, describes nearly every B2B brand, and gives a writer no
decision they can make differently because of it. If a dimension does not change a sentence,
cut it.

---

## 3. Tone shifts by context

Voice is constant, tone moves. A table of the contexts this brand actually writes in, and how
the dial moves in each.

Minimum contexts: sales or landing page, onboarding, support reply, bad news or apology,
social. Add whichever others they genuinely publish in.

For each: what moves, what stays fixed, and one example line.

**Fails when** it says tone should be "appropriate to the situation", which is not
instruction. Also fails when it lists contexts the client does not actually write in.

---

## 4. Messaging framework

The message architecture, so the same thing gets said the same way everywhere.

| Layer | What it is |
|---|---|
| Core message | One sentence. What this business is, for whom, and why it matters. |
| Pillars | Three or four themes everything ladders up to. |
| Proof points | Per pillar, what makes it credible. Facts, not adjectives. |
| Objection responses | The three things prospects push back on, and the honest answer. |

The core message must be consistent with the positioning in `13-brand-strategy.md`. If it
cannot be, the positioning is the problem and this document should say so rather than quietly
writing around it.

**Fails when** proof points are claims. "Industry-leading expertise" is not a proof point. A
named client outcome, a number, a credential, or a guarantee is.

---

## 5. Vocabulary

Two lists and a reason for each entry.

- **Words we use**: the specific terms this brand owns, including how they describe their own
  category and their customers.
- **Words we never use**: with the reason. Jargon they have outgrown, competitor language,
  industry terms their customers do not use, and anything that fails the voice dimensions.

Pull the customer-language side from `08-audience-persona.md` when it exists. How customers
describe the problem usually differs from how the business describes the product, and the
vocabulary list is where that gap gets closed.

**Fails when** the never-use list is only profanity and competitor names. The useful entries
are the comfortable filler words the client currently overuses: solutions, leverage, seamless,
robust, cutting-edge, passionate, journey.

---

## 6. Sentence and rhythm rules

The mechanical layer. Sentence length, paragraph length, contractions, person and tense,
oxford comma, sentence case or title case, numerals, how they handle lists, whether they ask
questions, how they open and close.

Also: **what this brand never does structurally.** Never opens with a rhetorical question,
never uses a one-word paragraph for emphasis, and so on.

**Fails when** it is a style guide copied from anywhere. These rules should come from reading
their best existing copy, and where the client has no good copy yet, say that this section is
prescriptive rather than descriptive.

---

## 7. Before and after

**The proof the document works.** Three to five real passages from the client's existing
copy, rewritten to spec, with a note on which rule drove each change.

Use their actual copy, never invented examples. Invented examples prove nothing because they
were written to be easy to fix.

**Fails when** the rewrites are better in ways the guide does not explain. If a change cannot
be traced to a dimension, a vocabulary entry or a rhythm rule, then the guide is incomplete
and the rewrite was done on instinct. Add the missing rule.

---

## 8. Naming and taglines

Only when in scope. Candidates with rationale, the shortlist, the recommendation, and the
checks run.

For names, state plainly what was and was not checked. A quick search is not a trademark
clearance, and the document must say so. Point them at a real search before they commit
anything to signage, packaging or a domain purchase.

**Fails when** a name is recommended without a stated availability check, or when a tagline is
scored on how it sounds read aloud by us rather than on what it claims.

---

## 9. What we could not establish

What we could not read, could not verify, or could not resolve. If the client had almost no
existing copy, this document is substantially prescriptive and that must be stated here, not
buried.

---

## Subject: company or founder

For a **founder voice**, the structure holds with three changes:

- Dimensions are descriptive before prescriptive. The goal is to capture how the person
  already writes and talks so ghostwritten copy passes as theirs, not to install a new voice.
  Work from transcripts, voice notes, sent emails and their own posts, in that order of value.
- Add **tells**: the specific verbal habits that make it recognisably them. Favourite
  constructions, the way they qualify a claim, how they disagree, what they find funny. These
  are what a reader notices when they go missing.
- Add **positions they hold**, so ghostwritten copy does not contradict something they have
  publicly said.

A founder voice guide that reads like a corporate voice guide has failed, because the whole
point is that it sounds like one specific person rather than a well-run company.
