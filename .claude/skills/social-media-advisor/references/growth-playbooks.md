# Growth playbooks — the account layer

Cross-platform. For per-platform mechanics use `platform-specs/`.

Four sections: **organic growth**, **engagement**, **personal branding**, **influencer and UGC**,
plus **listening and community**. They differ enormously in how well evidenced they are, and
each section says so at the top.

---

## 1. Organic account growth

**Evidence quality: good on mechanism, thin on tactics.**

### Start from the structural facts, not from tactics

Three findings should frame every growth conversation before any tactic is discussed:

- **Attention concentrates through cumulative advantage.** Preferential attachment produces
  heavy-tailed distributions in which a few accounts hold most of the attention, and this
  happens with or without platform favouritism `[C]` [s34]. The same inequality of interaction
  persists **across platforms** with different business models `[C]` [s56].
- **Virality is untied to audience size** `[C]` [s55].
- **A meaningful share of the outcome is luck**, given a recommendation process that allocates
  visibility on popularity bias `[C]` [s53].

**Say this to a client early.** It reframes slow growth from "our content is bad" to "this is
what the distribution looks like", which is both truer and more useful. It also stops them
buying the tactic that promises to beat a structural property of networks.

### What the growth literature actually supports

- **Tie formation is partly transitive**: follows propagate through existing connections
  `[C]` [s80], and follow behaviour has measurable predictors over time `[C]` [s77].
- **Communities have size and time dynamics**: online community formation and survival depend on
  group size and elapsed time `[C]` [s81][s79].
- **Audiences overlap across platforms** rather than being independent `[C]` [s2], which
  undercuts the "be everywhere" pitch: a second platform is often the same people again.
- **Followers are not one segment.** Different follower types engage differently `[C]` [s31].

### The interest-graph shift

Practitioner sources argue platforms have moved from ranking on the **social graph** (who you
follow) to the **interest graph** (what you engage with), with the tactical implication that
content should be tightly mapped to a topic so recommenders can classify it `[P]`.

This is directionally consistent with confirmed work showing rankers reallocate exposure away
from the follow graph `[C]` [s18][s37]. **The framing is practitioner; the underlying direction
is confirmed.** Say it that way.

### What has no support

Posting-frequency targets, follower thresholds, "post at this time", and any specific growth
rate. See `what-not-to-do.md`.

## 2. Engagement

**Evidence quality: strong, and mostly a warning.**

- **There is no standard engagement measure.** A systematic review finds wide heterogeneity, no
  dominant behavioural index, and studies that aggregate likes, comments and shares into one
  number `[C]` [s1]. **Two engagement rates are rarely comparable.**
- **Engagement is meta-analysed**: 196 effect sizes across 184 publications, n=146,380,
  resolving into an organic relationship-oriented pathway alongside a second pathway `[C]`
  [s72]. This is the largest evidence base in the corpus and it points at relationship, not
  trickery.
- **Liking and commenting have different drivers** `[C]` [s68], which supports measuring them
  separately and does **not** support any weighting ratio between them.
- **Engagement diverges from value.** "There is potentially a large gap between engagement
  signals and a desired notion of value that is worth optimizing for" `[C]` [s71]. This is the
  strongest argument available against making engagement the objective.
- **Engagement-optimised ranking amplifies divisive content** `[C]` [s45]. What performs is not
  the same as what is good for the brand.
- **Owned-social effectiveness research is divergent** and the meta-analysis exists to explain
  why rather than to resolve it `[C]` [s3]. **Never promise an engagement-to-sales rate.**

**The practical instruction:** name the business outcome first, then pick the engagement proxy
that plausibly ladders to it, then say out loud that it is a proxy.

## 3. Personal branding

**Evidence quality: this is the honest weak spot. There is a real literature and it is almost
entirely qualitative.**

What exists is a substantial body on **microcelebrity and self-branding** as social practice:
presenting as a public persona, treating followers as an audience, and performing authenticity
`[C]` [s14][s17]. There is a systematic review of personal branding as a field `[C]` [s19], work
on leaders' personal branding on professional platforms `[C]` [s22], on solo entrepreneurs'
self-presentation `[C]` [s94], on CEO communication style and parasocial interaction `[C]`
[s91][s93], and on impression management on enterprise platforms `[C]` [s92].

**What is missing is performance measurement.** These sources describe practices, motivations
and perceptions. They largely **do not report effect sizes**, and none establishes that personal
branding produces a business outcome at a given magnitude.

**Say:** "There's real research on personal branding, and it's about what people do and how it's
perceived rather than what it returns. Anyone quoting you a number for founder-led content is
not quoting this literature."

**Perceived authenticity** is the recurring mechanism `[C]` [s14][s4]: it is cultivated and
performed, not simply possessed. Useful framing for a client who wants to "be authentic" as a
tactic, since the literature treats authenticity as a craft with a cost, not a switch.

## 4. Influencer marketing and UGC

**Evidence quality: the strongest actionable section in this file. Two meta-analyses.**

- **Influencer marketing has significant positive effects** on attitudes, intentions and
  behaviours, with identified cross-study moderators `[C]` [s9][s6][s12].
- **The mechanisms are source credibility and parasocial relationship** `[C]` [s9][s4][s21].
  Not reach. This is the actionable part: select on credibility and audience relationship, not
  on follower count.
- **Follower count is non-monotonic.** Mid-tier outperforms the largest accounts `[C]` [s5], and
  influencer size functions as a moderator `[C]` [s9][s20]. **Refuse follower-count sorting when
  a client is picking influencers.**
- **Disclosure has a real cost, and it is mitigable.** Sponsorship disclosure reduces perceived
  source credibility and brand attitudes, which lowers purchase intention, **but a strong
  parasocial relationship attenuates that effect** `[C]` [s10][s15].

**Never advise concealing a paid relationship.** Disclosure is a legal requirement, and the
finding is an argument for choosing influencers with genuine audience relationships, not for
hiding the arrangement.

## 5. Listening and community management

**Evidence quality: craft only, and read the note below before using this section.**

**Provenance note, stated because it affects how much to trust this section.** The original
research pass for this topic was contaminated: its query led with the words "social listening"
and the engines returned the Social Security Administration, two dictionaries and a bar. Ten of
twelve sources were junk. The pass was rewritten around "brand mention monitoring" and
"community manager" and re-run, and the junk was purged from the corpus. What remains is a
genuine but **entirely craft-tier** set of tool comparisons and workflow guides `[K]`
[s329][s332][s333][s334][s335][s336][s337][s190][s128].

`[K]` may never support a factual claim. So:

- **Tooling**: free and paid brand-monitoring tools are well catalogued `[K]`
  [s332][s334][s335]. Treat as a starting list, verify current capability before recommending.
- **Workflow**: the practitioner consensus is a combined approach, platform-native inboxes for
  high-value mentions, third-party tooling for scale, and an explicit triage rule prioritising
  safety and speed `[K]` [s128].
- **What the corpus does NOT cover**, and should be said plainly when asked: cross-platform
  competitive monitoring specifics, large-scale comment and DM triage mechanics, moderation and
  escalation playbooks, public crisis response examples, employee advocacy programme design, and
  **how share of voice is calculated and where it misleads**. All were asked for; none came back
  usable.

For community building beyond monitoring, route to `marketing-skills/community-marketing` and
label it as generic rather than research-backed.
