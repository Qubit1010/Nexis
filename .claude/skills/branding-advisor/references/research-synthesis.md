# Branding Research Synthesis — 2026

The cited master document behind `branding-advisor` and the three `brand-*` execution skills.

**The corpus:** 260 sources, **132 confirmed** (peer-reviewed or primary data) against **128
practitioner** (agency, vendor, or consultancy making a case for its own service). Built from
16 deep passes through the in-repo `research` skill. Inline `[sN]` resolves to `sources[N-1]`
in `_research/sources.json`. `[C]` = confirmed, `[P]` = practitioner.

**How to read this.** Branding is the most folklore-heavy subject in this repo. A large share
of what circulates as branding fact is either a vendor survey with the attribution stripped or
a number with no traceable origin at all. Every section below therefore separates three
things: what the empirical literature actually shows, what practitioners assert without
evidence, and what nobody has established. **The third category is the most valuable one
here.** Where a section says a number is not available, do not supply one.

**A recurring limitation, stated once.** Many peer-reviewed sources in this corpus were
retrieved as abstracts or gated pages. The corpus therefore establishes *that* a relationship
was studied and in which direction, far more often than it establishes *how large* the effect
is. Effect sizes are mostly absent. Claiming one from this corpus is a fabrication.

## Q1 — Brand identity models

**The three named models are managerial frameworks, not validated instruments.** Aaker's brand
identity planning model, Kapferer's brand identity prism and Keller's CBBE pyramid are
described consistently across practitioner sources `[P]` [s139] [s140] [s143] [s154] [s170],
which supply components and steps but **no peer-reviewed empirical tests, effect sizes, or
quantified business impacts** for any of the three.

Be careful in both directions. The corpus does not validate these models, and it also **does
not substantiate the claim that they are untestable consultant scaffolding**. That criticism
circulates but is not evidenced here. The honest position is that they are useful structuring
devices whose predictive validity has not been demonstrated in this source set.

**What is empirically established is the surrounding vocabulary:**

| Construct | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|
| Brand equity | Multidimensional consumer-based measurement has been developed and validated `[C]` [s52], and brand equity is linked to customer acquisition, retention and profit margin `[C]` [s55] |
| Brand image | A measurement construct with defined operationalization issues `[C]` [s53]; validated multi-dimensional scales exist `[C]` [s56] |
| Brand attitude | Distinct from image, and both feed equity `[C]` [s51] |
| Corporate reputation | A separate stakeholder-level construct `[C]` [s54] |
| Organizational identity | A social-actor conception, distinct again `[C]` [s57] |
| Brand meaning | Reviewed as its own research stream `[C]` [s59] |

**The practically useful distinction**, and the one to give a client: identity is what the firm
projects, image is what the customer perceives, equity is the commercial value of that
perception, reputation is the accumulated judgment across stakeholders. The gap between the
first two is what brand work exists to close.

**Not in sources:** any test of whether using Aaker, Kapferer or Keller produces better
outcomes than not using them.

## Q2 — Brand positioning

**No causal evidence that clearly positioned brands outperform ambiguously positioned ones.**
This is the single most important honesty flag in the corpus, because it contradicts what
every positioning deck asserts. Practitioner sources claim the performance benefit
consistently `[P]` [s148] [s196] [s206] [s216] and none of them report primary data.

What *is* peer-reviewed is the machinery around positioning:

- Positioning-strategy effectiveness has been evaluated from a consumer perspective `[C]` [s61].
- Customer-perceived positioning effectiveness has been conceptualized and operationalized as
  a measurable construct `[C]` [s62].
- Positioning options for achieving brand association have a psychological account `[C]` [s63].
- Antecedents and consequences of effective brand positioning have been studied `[C]` [s64].
- Decision-support models exist for selecting a benefit position `[C]` [s66], and product
  design strategies for target-market positioning are established `[C]` [s67].

**The counter-current.** Perceived brand differentiation may matter less than positioning
practice assumes `[C]` [s1], and brand image associations follow a natural monopoly pattern in
which larger brands simply own more associations `[C]` [s2]. Both come from the
Ehrenberg-Bass tradition. See Q11, where this argument is handled in full.

**Perceptual maps** are practitioner-standard `[P]` [s197] [s213], sometimes grounded in
multidimensional scaling. Treat the output as a conversation structure, not a measurement.

**Not in sources:** category entry points, despite the term's currency.

## Q3 — Brand personality and archetypes

**This section contains the corpus's cleanest true-versus-folklore split, and the skill should
lead with it whenever archetypes come up.**

**Aaker's brand personality scale is real research.** Published in *Journal of Marketing
Research* `[C]` [s69], five dimensions (Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication,
Ruggedness). It has an active literature: current insights and future directions `[C]` [s72],
emotional brand attachment relative importance `[C]` [s71], and cross-cultural structure work
comparing the US and Korea that finds **common and culture-specific dimensions** `[C]` [s73].
Scale design and validation continues in other languages `[C]` [s10]. A longitudinal panel
studies how the personality-to-equity effect changed over 18 years `[C]` [s9], though the
accessible material does not give its coefficients.

**Self-congruity**, the fit between consumer self-concept and brand personality, has
moderators identified `[C]` [s70] and has been compared conceptually and by measurement against
brand personality `[C]` [s75]. Humanizing and personification effects are studied `[C]` [s74],
with cultural factors moderating personification strategy effectiveness `[C]` [s78].

**Jungian archetypes are not in the same category.** Practitioner sources present the 12
archetypes as strategic tools and attribute them to Jung's collective unconscious, claiming
they "transcend cultural boundaries", with **no empirical tests, effect sizes or peer-reviewed
citations** `[P]` [s169]. Two confirmed sources do engage archetypes: one examines advertising
between archetype and brand personality `[C]` [s76], and one explicitly **rethinks archetypal
branding** and investigates authenticity effects `[C]` [s77]. That is a small literature
interrogating the idea, not a validation of the 12-type system.

**The position to take:** archetypes are a creative alignment device. They help a team agree on
a feel and brief a designer. They are not evidence, they do not predict, and presenting them
to a client as research-backed is a misrepresentation.

**Not in sources:** effect sizes for congruence on preference.

## Q4 — Brand voice, tone and messaging

**Practitioner voice frameworks have no empirical backing in this corpus.** Every agency source
`[P]` [s185] [s193] [s210] [s220] [s222] supplies steps and examples, and none supplies an
experiment, an effect size, or a performance comparison between registers.

**But brand language does have a real research literature**, and it does not say what the
agency sources assume:

- **Informal brand communication can backfire.** "Don't pretend to be my friend!" studies when
  an informal register damages rather than helps `[C]` [s79]. This is the most decision-relevant
  finding in the section and it directly contradicts the default "be conversational" advice.
- **Conversational human voice** has measured effects, including on observers of a
  conversation rather than only its participant `[C]` [s81].
- **Brand linguistics** is an established theory-driven framework for studying language in
  branding `[C]` [s82], with a systematic review of brand language on social media `[C]` [s80].
- **Organizational personification and personality** affect response `[C]` [s84], and
  linguistic practices in brand conversation have been analyzed `[C]` [s85] [s14].
- Leaders' messages in corporate communication have identifiable move structures and styles
  `[C]` [s86].
- Consumer engagement with brand posts is studied at the post level `[C]` [s83].

**The practical translation:** a voice spec should be defensible as a fit to audience and
context, not as a universal best practice. "More casual performs better" is not supported. The
evidence supports the opposite risk existing.

**Not in sources:** messaging houses and message hierarchies as tested structures; how writers
actually adopt voice guidelines in practice.

## Q5 — Brand story and narrative

**The best-evidenced section in the corpus.** Narrative transportation is a genuine research
programme, not a consultant metaphor.

- **Meta-analytic support exists.** A multimethod meta-analysis of verbal persuasion in
  marketing `[C]` [s87], and a meta-analysis of storytelling in the digital era identifying
  moderators `[C]` [s88].
- **Narrative versus factual ad copy** has been tested directly on product evaluation `[C]`
  [s89], and narratives are established in advertising for experiential services `[C]` [s90].
- A systematic literature review of narrative transportation maps the field `[C]` [s92], with
  further work relating transportation to consumer outcomes `[C]` [s93], mini-film persuasion
  `[C]` [s94], and CSR narrative persuasion `[C]` [s95].
- **Narrative perspective matters.** First-person versus third-person framing changes impact
  `[C]` [s6], which is a directly actionable lever when writing a brand story.
- **Founder stories specifically:** founders' stories influence brand authenticity, and the
  mechanism runs through values `[C]` [s91]. This is the citation that justifies the brand-story
  section of a deliverable, and it is the strongest founder-related finding in the corpus.

**What this does not license.** No primary data on StoryBrand or any other commercial narrative
framework `[P]`. No support for "stories are 22 times more memorable than facts" or any variant
,  such statistics are not traceable in this corpus and must not be repeated.

## Q6 — Naming and taglines

**Sound symbolism is real.** Specific speech sounds systematically shape consumer judgment, and
successful brand names show non-random phonetic patterns `[C]` [s100] [s101] [s102].

- Brand spelling influences memory for auditorily presented names `[C]` [s103].
- Figurativeness of names and logos affects memory `[C]` [s96].
- **Difficult-to-pronounce names carry measured liabilities** `[C]` [s99], and **company name
  fluency relates to investor recognition and firm value** `[C]` [s98]. Pronounceability is the
  most defensible single naming criterion in this corpus.
- Whether marketing affects the value of corporate names has been studied directly `[C]` [s4].

**Taglines have exactly one solid finding here**, and it is oddly specific: **a brief pause
between a tagline and the brand name increases brand name recognition** `[C]` [s97]. That is
the entire evidenced tagline literature in this corpus. Everything else about taglines is
practitioner judgment.

**Descriptive versus suggestive versus arbitrary** names, and the trademark-protectability
trade-off between them, are practitioner-sourced `[P]` [s205] [s237]. The trade-off is real as
a legal matter but is not empirically ranked here.

**Not in sources:** tagline recall research beyond [s97]; financial market effects of name
changes at the level of extractable numbers (see Q12 for the rebranding side).

## Q7 — Colour in branding

**The 80% claim is the flagship falsehood of this subject and the corpus settles it.** Two
independent reviews trace the provenance of "colour increases brand recognition by 80%" and
conclude it is **untraceable to any credible primary source**, and should be treated as a myth
`[P]` [s144] [s147]. Statistics-aggregator pages repeat it and similar figures with no primary
citation `[P]` [s141].

**What is actually established:**

- **Hue maps to brand personality.** Red associates with excitement, blue with competence
  `[C]` [s107] (Labrecque & Milne, *JAMS*).
- **Colour appropriateness matters more than hue alone.** Fit between colour and
  brand/product is the more consequential variable, and saturation and value matter alongside
  hue `[C]` [s104] [s110].
- **Category colour norms exist**, and atypical packaging colour has measured consequences
  `[C]` [s112].
- **Trust is colour-sensitive** `[C]` [s113], and colour affects cognitive task performance
  `[C]` [s109] (Mehta & Zhu, *Science*).
- **Context moderates everything.** Red's influence depends on psychological context `[C]`
  [s111], and the broader colour-and-psychological-functioning literature is reviewed `[C]`
  [s13].
- **Cross-cultural variation** is reviewed `[C]` [s105], and colour research in marketing has
  documented theoretical and technical measurement problems `[C]` [s106].
- Colour preference and familiarity affect brand logo recall `[C]` [s108].

**Effect sizes are not available** in the accessible material for any of the above. Say the
direction, not the magnitude.

**Not in sources:** accessibility and contrast requirements. Those come from WCAG, which is a
standard rather than a finding, and `brand-visual` computes contrast rather than citing it.

## Q8 — Typography

**Typeface semantics are established.** Typefaces carry semantic associations that transfer to
the message `[C]` [s114] [s118], and typeface design is usable for impression management
`[C]` [s115]. Typographic factors affect advertising persuasion `[C]` [s117]. Serif versus
sans-serif effects on consumer perception have been studied directly `[C]` [s116].

**Legibility is contested and should be presented that way.** The corpus carries several
legibility sources `[C]` [s119] [s120] [s121] [s122], and practitioner reviews conclude there is
**no objective, universally applicable serif-versus-sans legibility rule** `[P]` [s199] [s207].
Anyone asserting one is overreaching.

**Two practitioner numbers circulate and neither is verified here:**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| A Monotype study showing typeface choice raises positive consumer response by up to 13% | Reported secondhand by a vendor `[P]` [s160]. No primary study in this corpus. Do not cite. |
| Baskerville rated more believable than Comic Sans in a New York Times study | Reported secondhand `[P]` [s189]. Widely repeated. Not verified here. |

**Not in sources:** disfluency effects, variable fonts and web performance, and **font
licensing models**. Licensing is a live commercial risk for clients and the corpus is silent
on it, so `brand-visual` must check licences case by case and never state a cost from memory.

## Q9 — Logo design

A strong evidence base, and one of the corpus's best-supported sections.

- **Henderson & Cote's guidelines for selecting or modifying logos** `[C]` [s123], the
  foundational work.
- **Descriptive logos**: "Let the Logo Do the Talking" tests descriptiveness directly `[C]`
  [s124]. A widely circulated HBR summary asserts descriptive marks improve evaluations and
  performance but supplies no numbers `[P]`.
- **Complexity** moderates response `[C]` [s125], and visual versus conceptual complexity
  dimensions have been separated `[C]` [s127].
- **Shape**: rounding angular logos shifts brand attitude `[C]` [s126]; asymmetry interacts with
  brand personality `[C]` [s129]; shape has a documented impact on consumer inference `[C]`
  [s11]. Naturalness affects response `[P]` [s215]. Orientation and implied motion affect
  perceived reliability and innovativeness `[C]` [s15].
- **Firm-level**: the role of brand logos in firm performance has been studied `[C]` [s130], as
  has logo change on brand attitude `[C]` [s131].
- **Redesign risk is real.** Logo-shape redesign influences loyalty and repurchase through
  brand attitude as a mediator `[P]` [s198], and public backlash to redesign is documented
  (the Gap 2010 case) `[C]` [s11].

**Critical constraint: no "X% improvement" statistic about logo effects is traceable to a
primary study in this corpus.** Direction yes, magnitude no. A client asking "how much will a
new logo lift sales" gets an honest "no credible figure exists", not an estimate.

## Q10 and Q16 — Brand guidelines, design systems and corporate visual identity

Two passes cover this; Q16 was remedial after Q10 returned mostly agency comparison posts.

**Whether guidelines improve consistency is not established.** Evidence in the practitioner
sources is anecdotal, with no effect sizes `[P]` [s178] [s248]. This matters because it is the
implicit promise of the deliverable.

**Why guidelines fail is better documented.** Many brand documents are print-first and lack
actionable digital guidance, producing a collaboration gap and non-adoption by product teams
`[P]` [s181] [s190]. One practitioner analysis addresses why brand manuals fail specifically at
defining colour `[P]` [s253]. Governance tension over where brand style ends and the design
system begins is a recurring theme `[P]` [s181].

**Design systems have genuine peer-reviewed research**, mostly from software engineering and
HCI rather than marketing:

- Understanding and supporting the design-systems practice, empirically `[C]` [s18].
- Scaling UX with design systems `[C]` [s21].
- Improving design-system adoption via inner source `[C]` [s17].
- Style tokens as the consistency mechanism `[C]` [s19].
- Conceptual clarification of what "design system" even means `[C]` [s20].
- Bridging brand strategy and design through a brand experience manual `[C]` [s22].

**Corporate visual identity (CVI) is the academic name for this subject** and carries the
findings that matter most:

- **Consistency is a dogma worth questioning.** One paper explicitly explores "the dogma of
  consistency" in CVI `[C]` [s46]. Consistency is an assumption in practice, not a proven law.
- **Managing CVI differs across organizations** `[C]` [s44], and **employee response is the
  hinge**: corporate identity management shapes employee responses `[C]` [s45] [s50], with
  internal-perspective case work `[C]` [s48].
- Olins' visual identity structure has been tested for salience against UK companies `[C]` [s47].
- Art directors' own understanding of visual brand identity has been studied `[C]` [s49].
- Brand identity driven by consumers is a distinct effect `[C]` [s16].

**The practical conclusion:** a guidelines document is necessary but not sufficient. Adoption
is an organizational problem, and the evidence points at employees and governance rather than
at the document's completeness. A more beautiful PDF does not fix non-adoption.

## Q11 — Distinctive assets versus differentiation

**Preserve this disagreement. Do not resolve it.** It is live, both sides have evidence, and
flattening it to make a tidier deliverable is the failure mode.

**The Ehrenberg-Bass position:** distinctive brand assets are learned, non-name cues that help
people notice, recognize and recall a brand; being *identifiable* beats being *meaningfully
unique* `[C]` [s7] [s8]. Supporting empirical work in this tradition:

- **Perceived brand differentiation may matter less than assumed** `[C]` [s1].
- **Natural monopoly effect**: larger brands hold more image associations, so association
  counts partly reflect size rather than positioning skill `[C]` [s2].
- **Double jeopardy** as an empirical generalization `[C]` [s24], with awareness replication and
  extension work `[C]` [s3].
- **Brand salience** and memory retrieval: salience effects in brand recall `[C]` [s43],
  competition for memory retrieval between private label and national brands `[C]` [s26], and
  neuroscientific foundations proposed as "three laws of branding" `[C]` [s25].
- Marketer judgment and consumer perception of branding strength can be compared `[C]` [s23].

**The differentiation side** is Q2's evidence base: positioning effectiveness is measurable
`[C]` [s61] [s62] [s64] and positioning options have psychological grounding `[C]` [s63].

**The reconciliation to give a client**: presented as reasoning, not as a finding: the two
answer different questions. Distinctiveness governs whether a buyer *retrieves and recognizes*
you at the moment of choice. Differentiation governs whether they have a *reason to pick* you
once retrieved. A brand with no distinctive assets is not in the consideration set to begin
with; a brand with distinctive assets and no reason to choose competes only on price and
availability. Small brands generally have a bigger distinctiveness deficit than a
differentiation deficit, which is why the Ehrenberg-Bass emphasis is usually the more useful
starting point for an SMB.

**Note honestly:** quantitative detail, effect sizes and mental-availability metrics are **not
extractable** from the accessible material `[C]` gap. Several widely-cited Ehrenberg-Bass
figures circulate; this corpus does not verify them.

## Q12 — Rebranding risk

A well-populated section, and the evidence supports caution.

- **Name changes**: what leads a firm to change its name and what follows `[C]` [s27]; effects
  of corporate name changes on the firm information environment `[C]` [s30]; whether marketing
  affects corporate name value `[C]` [s4]; a nexus study on rebranding and stock market
  performance `[C]` [s34].
- **Consumer reaction**: to service rebranding `[C]` [s28]; moderated by identification, where
  **high identifiers react differently from low identifiers** `[C]` [s29]. That is the mechanism
  behind backlash, the most loyal customers object most.
- **Package redesign**: preservation, rejuvenation or confusion `[C]` [s32]. Changing what
  people recognize carries a recognition cost.
- **Socially motivated rebrands** have measured consumer responses `[C]` [s31], including a
  specific study of pancake-mix preference after the Aunt Jemima change `[C]` [s35].
- **Brand architecture** implications of corporate rebranding `[C]` [s33].
- **Internal perspective**: employee and stakeholder dynamics determine success `[P]` [s226],
  consistent with the CVI findings in Q16.

**Refresh versus rebrand** decision frameworks are practitioner-sourced `[P]` [s180] [s239]
[s241] [s243], converging on a warning against cosmetic change not grounded in real business
change. That convergence is judgment, not evidence, but it is consistent with the confirmed
risk literature.

**Not in sources:** any fixed rebrand failure rate. **No "X% of rebrands fail" statistic is
supported by primary data here.** Extractable abnormal returns from the event studies are also
not available in the accessible material.

## Q13 and Q15 — Personal and founder branding

**This is the weakest section in the corpus and the skill must say so.**

**Q13 returned nothing usable.** All ten results were LinkedIn profiles of people who sell
personal-branding services. The search engines read the popular phrasing as a request to find
practitioners rather than research. Every result was correctly filtered out, leaving the pass
with **zero sources**.

**Q15 re-asked in academic register** (CEO celebrity, executive reputation, human brands,
impression management) and recovered a little, but not much: **one confirmed source**,
celebrity CEOs and firm innovation investment `[C]` [s5], plus practitioner and mirror-hosted
material on celebrity-CEO credibility and firm performance `[P]` [s134] [s135] [s137] and the
costs and benefits of CEO reputation `[P]` [s137].

**What this means in practice.** The honest statement to a client is that founder-brand work
rests on judgment and analogy, not on an evidence base comparable to logo, colour, naming or
narrative work. Two adjacent findings do transfer and should be used:

- **Founder stories influence brand authenticity through values** `[C]` [s91] (Q5). This is the
  strongest evidenced anchor for founder-brand work in the whole corpus and it belongs in the
  brand story, not in a posting schedule.
- **Personification and self-congruity research** `[C]` [s74] [s70] [s75] (Q3) transfers
  partially, since a founder brand is a person rather than a personified company.

**Key-person concentration risk**, the exposure created when a company's identity depends on
one individual, is **not evidenced in this corpus**. It remains a reasoned argument, and must
be labelled as such rather than presented as a finding.

## Q14 — Brand measurement

**Brand equity measurement is a mature research area.**

- Consumer-based brand equity conceptualization and measurement, reviewed `[C]` [s36], with a
  separate assessment of competing measures `[C]` [s37] and a foundational treatment of what
  brand equity is and how to measure it `[C]` [s39].
- **Revenue premium as an outcome measure** `[C]` [s41], the most concrete, business-legible
  operationalization in the corpus.
- **Awareness dynamics**: sources and effects of brand awareness disentangled `[C]` [s40];
  brand and advertising awareness replication `[C]` [s3].
- **Salience is distinct from awareness**: salience effects in brand recall `[C]` [s43]. Use the
  distinction; it changes what gets measured.
- **Search as a signal**: brand attitudes relate to search engine queries `[C]` [s42]. This is
  the confirmed anchor under "share of search", which itself appears only as practitioner and
  thesis material `[P]` [s255].
- **Brand valuation** methods are catalogued `[C]` [s38], with practitioner treatments `[P]`
  [s224].

**The one number practitioners quote here is a vendor statistic:** "over 85% of B2B
decision-makers are inclined to shortlist vendors they recognize and trust" `[P]` [s151]. Not
peer-reviewed. Do not present it as research.

**Not in sources:** sample-size requirements for brand tracking. A small business running a
20-response survey is producing a directional signal, not a percentage, and the deliverable
must say so rather than reporting a number to two decimal places.
