# Deliverable Structure — `13-brand-strategy.md`

Section order for the brand strategy document. Written for the client, not for us.

Every section below carries a **fails when** line. That is the thing to check before
sending, and it is usually the difference between a brand strategy and a page of adjectives.

---

## 0. What we know

The house opener. A `| Fact | Source | Confidence |` table covering everything the rest of
the document rests on.

- **High** = verified in two places, or primary (their own numbers, their own site, a
  recorded call).
- **Medium** = single source.
- **Low** = inferred by us.
- Anything the client told us is labelled **client-reported**, never promoted above Medium.

**Fails when** the table is decoration and the body then states things it does not support.
If a claim is not in this table, it does not belong in section 2 as a fact.

---

## 1. Brand at a glance

One page. Essence, position, personality, promise. Written so someone who reads only this
page can brief a designer or a writer without misleading them.

Write this **last**, from the finished sections. Writing it first produces a summary the
document then has to live up to.

**Fails when** it would fit any competitor with the name swapped.

---

## 2. Brand positioning

The core of the document. Needs all five, and each one is a claim someone could dispute:

| Part | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Category / frame of reference | What are we competing to be chosen instead of? |
| Target | Who is this for, specifically? |
| Point of difference | What do we offer that the alternatives do not? |
| Reasons to believe | Why should anyone believe that? |
| Position statement | The whole thing in one sentence |

The frame of reference is the part most often skipped and it changes everything downstream.
"The best project management tool" and "the alternative to hiring a project manager" are
different businesses with different competitors.

Pull the market position and UVP from `07-strategic-foundation.md` when it exists. **Do not
re-derive them.** If they conflict with what the brand work suggests, say so explicitly and
name which one you changed and why. Silently contradicting the strategy document is how a
client ends up with two artifacts that disagree.

**Fails when** the point of difference is a feature list, a quality claim, or something every
competitor also says. "Quality, service and value" is the canonical failure.

---

## 3. Brand personality

How the brand behaves, expressed so a writer and a designer can both act on it.

Use **traits with behavioural definitions**, not adjectives. "Direct" is an adjective.
"Direct: gives the answer in the first sentence, does not pad bad news" is a definition
someone can follow.

If an archetype is used, it goes here with an explicit honesty label. Archetypes are a
creative alignment device, not evidence, and the document must say so rather than implying a
research pedigree the framework does not have. See `what-not-to-do.md`.

**Fails when** the traits are three positive adjectives no company would reject. If the
opposite trait is obviously undesirable, the trait carries no information. "Trustworthy" fails
this test. "Blunt" passes it, because a brand could reasonably choose "diplomatic" instead.

---

## 4. Brand promise

The one thing a customer can count on every time. Specific enough that failing to deliver it
would be noticeable.

**Fails when** it is aspirational rather than operational. A promise the business has no
mechanism to keep is a liability, not a brand asset. If they cannot describe how they deliver
it on a bad week, it is not their promise.

---

## 5. Brand story

Origin, the problem the founder kept running into, why the business exists, what changed. The
narrative a person would actually tell.

Keep it true. An invented or embellished founding story is the single highest-risk item in
this document: it is client-facing, quotable, and checkable.

**Fails when** it is a company timeline. Founded, expanded, opened a second office. That is a
history, not a story, and nobody has ever been persuaded by one.

---

## 6. Brand values

Three to five, each expressed as a behaviour with a **trade-off attached**. A value that costs
nothing is not a value.

Format: the value, what it means in practice, and what the business gives up to hold it.

**Fails when** the list is interchangeable with any other company's. Integrity, innovation,
teamwork, excellence. If the values do not constrain a hiring or pricing decision, they are
wallpaper and should be cut rather than shipped.

---

## 7. What this rules out

The section that makes the rest of the document real. Name the customers this brand is not
for, the work it will not take, the tone it will not use, and the positions it is
deliberately conceding to competitors.

A strategy with no exclusions has not made any choices. This section is where you find out
whether sections 2-6 were decisions or descriptions.

**Fails when** it is empty, hedged, or lists only things nobody would want anyway.

---

## 8. Assumptions to validate

Everything in the document tagged Low confidence in section 0, plus every inference we made
about their customers that came from us rather than from data. Each with how to check it.

**Fails when** it is a formality. If the list is short, the document is probably overconfident
rather than well-researched.

---

## 9. What we could not establish

Plainly stated. What we tried, why it did not resolve, and what would resolve it.

**Fails when** it is omitted because the document looks stronger without it. It does not. A
brand strategy with no stated gaps reads as unexamined to anyone who knows the category.

---

## Subject: company or founder

When the subject is a **founder** rather than a company, the sections hold but shift:

| Section | Founder version |
|---|---|
| 2. Positioning | What they are known for, against which other voices in the space |
| 3. Personality | How they come across, including the parts they will not perform |
| 4. Promise | What a reader reliably gets from following them |
| 5. Story | Their actual path, including what did not work |
| 6. Values | Positions they will take publicly that cost them something |
| 7. Rules out | Topics they will not cover, audiences they are not for |

Add one section a company brand does not need:

**7b. Separation from the company brand.** How the personal brand relates to the business:
what is shared, what is deliberately distinct, and what happens to the company if the person
steps back. Tying a company's whole identity to one individual is a concentration risk and
the document should name it rather than treat visibility as costless.

Content pillars, posting cadence and platform strategy are **not** in this document. That is
`marketing-advisor` and `content-engine`. This defines who the person is; those decide where
and how often they show up.
