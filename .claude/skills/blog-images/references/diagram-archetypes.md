# Diagram archetypes

A catalogue of diagram *kinds*, what each is good at, and when it is the wrong choice. Pick by what
the article argues, not by what looks good.

**Read `signature-ledger.md` first.** An archetype already used on another article is unavailable
for a cover, however well it fits.

## The choosing question

> What is the single claim this article makes that a reader would argue with?

Draw that. Not the topic, the claim. "AI agents versus skills" is a topic and produces a generic
two-column table. "An agent's failure propagates while a skill's does not" is a claim and produces
a drawing.

---

## Comparison shapes

**Two facing panels.** Same frame, two subjects, one accented. Best when the article's whole
argument is a contrast and both sides need equal weight. Weak when one side is obviously correct,
because the symmetry implies a fairness the argument does not have.

**A rail versus a branching tree.** Two path topologies from one start. Best for fixed-versus-adaptive
arguments where the *shape* of the difference is the point.

**A balance or lever.** Two loads, one fulcrum. Best for tradeoffs with a genuine exchange rate.
Avoid when nothing is actually being traded.

## Quantity and scale shapes

**A calibrated ladder.** A vertical axis with bands. Best for price tiers, maturity levels, or
anything where the reader needs to locate themselves on a scale.

**A gauge with a dead zone.** A reading plus a region where the reading means nothing. Best for
measurement-integrity arguments, which are common and almost never drawn.

**A side-on stack.** Layers with a waterline. Best when the argument is "you are only being shown
the top layer."

## Process shapes

**A station pipeline.** A track with stops, each doing one thing. Best when the article walks
through stages and each stage has its own failure.

**A funnel.** Narrowing stages with loss between them. Overused, so reserve it for genuine
attrition, never for a generic process.

**A timeline with a break.** Sequence where one event changes the rule. Best for "this changed on
date X" arguments.

## Structure shapes

**Nested containment.** Boxes inside boxes. Best for scope and ownership arguments.

**A map with a boundary.** Territory split by a line. Best for in-house versus outsourced, or
anything where the line's position is the argument.

**A switchboard.** Inputs routed to outputs. Best for routing and orchestration.

**A ledger that reconciles.** Two columns that must balance. Best for cost, accounting and
provenance arguments.

---

## Supporting figures

Supporting figures follow different rules from covers.

- They are **not** required to be a unique archetype. A supporting figure can reuse a shape, because it lives inside the article's context rather than representing it.
- Each one **must map to a named H2 in its own article** and reuse that section's actual terms. A figure introducing a concept the article does not discuss is decoration.
- Prefer a table-shaped figure or a small annotated sequence. Supporting figures carry more text than covers and that is correct: the reader is already reading.

## The two failure modes

**Decoration.** The image looks like the topic but carries no claim. Test: cover the title. If the
image still says something arguable, it is a diagram. If it just says "technology", delete it.

**Restating the paragraph.** The image repeats the sentence beside it in boxes. A figure should let
a reader skip the paragraph, not duplicate it.
